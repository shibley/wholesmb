Businesses for Sale in Minneapolis, MN: How to Find and Evaluate Them

The Twin Cities are one of the better small-business acquisition markets in the country for an unglamorous reason: an unusually dense base of large corporate headquarters supports a deep layer of B2B suppliers and service firms underneath it, and a lot of those firms are owned by people approaching retirement. That combination — real contracted revenue plus a genuine succession pipeline — is what a buyer wants. What you have to price properly is the Minnesota layer: a meaningful state tax and compliance stack, a paid leave program and earned sick time, sales tax rates that differ across the metro, and a winter that turns several whole industries into seasonal businesses. This guide covers where Twin Cities listings live, which categories trade, what to verify, and how these deals get financed.

Where businesses for sale in Minneapolis are listed

BizBuySell carries the largest public inventory for the metro, but a search restricted to the city of Minneapolis will miss most of it. This is a two-downtown, heavily suburban region, and the listing volume reflects that. St. Paul is a separate market with its own broker relationships rather than a suburb of Minneapolis. The southwest and west suburbs — Bloomington, Edina, Eden Prairie, Minnetonka, Plymouth — hold a large share of the professional-service, B2B, and medical-adjacent listings. Maple Grove and the northwest suburbs, and Woodbury, Eagan, and Burnsville to the east and south, cover much of the trades and consumer-service inventory at more accessible price points.

The Twin Cities also have a deep and long-established intermediary community. A real share of Main Street deals here move through local business brokers, CPAs, and transaction attorneys before they ever reach a public portal, and in the B2B and light-manufacturing categories that share is higher still. If you know the category you want, telling three or four local accountants and a couple of brokers exactly what you are looking for is a genuinely productive use of a week. Popular industries for sale in the Twin Cities

The listing mix reflects a corporate-headquarters economy, a large healthcare and medical device sector, an older housing stock, and a manufacturing base that stayed:

  • B2B and professional services — accounting firms, insurance agencies, staffing agencies, and managed IT. This is the category the headquarters base actually creates, and it carries the most contracted, most financeable revenue in the metro.
  • Healthcare and practices — dental, veterinary, and chiropractic practices, plus home care agencies and med spas.
  • Building trades — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and roofing. A cold-climate housing stock makes heating, envelope, and ice-related work structurally recurring rather than discretionary, and hail and storm exposure keeps roofing volume high.
  • Recurring-service businesses — commercial cleaning, landscaping paired with snow removal, pest control, and property management.
  • Light manufacturing and machining — machine shops, contract fabrication, and medical-device suppliers, concentrated in the suburban industrial corridors. Frequently the most interesting listings in the market and the ones first-time buyers overlook.
  • Consumer and food service — restaurants, bars, coffee shops, salons, gyms, and auto repair.

Twin Cities submarkets to watch

Downtown Minneapolis and downtown St. Paul — both remain shaped by office attendance patterns that have not returned to their pre-2020 shape. A business whose revenue depends on weekday office workers deserves a much harder look at its most recent twelve months than at its historical peak, and skyway-level retail and lunch-service operations are the clearest example. Evening and residential-driven businesses in the same buildings have generally held up better.

The southwest and west suburbs — Edina, Bloomington, Eden Prairie, Minnetonka, Plymouth — carry the highest household incomes and the densest concentration of corporate offices. This is where B2B services, professional practices, and trades with commercial client bases sit, and where a seller is most likely to own the building, which opens the door to an SBA 504 structure on the real estate.

The north and northwest — Maple Grove, Brooklyn Park, Blaine, Coon Rapids — is growing residentially and is where a lot of the consumer-service and residential-trades inventory sits at more accessible asking prices. The east and south — Woodbury, Eagan, Burnsville, Apple Valley — is a similar profile with a St. Paul-facing labor market.

Watch the sales tax boundaries. Minnesota levies a state sales and use tax, and local jurisdictions in the metro layer additional taxes on top of it, so the combined rate can differ between Minneapolis, St. Paul, and neighboring suburbs. For a retail or restaurant business, that difference affects both the seller’s historical filings and your own pricing, and it is worth confirming the exact current rate for the specific address rather than assuming a metro-wide figure.

How to evaluate a Minneapolis business listing

Start with revenue verification through sales tax filings. Minnesota’s sales and use tax gives you a verification source many states with no sales tax do not: for any retail, restaurant, or taxable-service business, the filed state sales tax returns are a revenue floor the seller had every incentive not to overstate. Reconcile them against bank deposits, merchant processor statements, and filed federal returns. If reported revenue materially exceeds the sales-tax base, get an explanation before proceeding.

Then work the employment compliance stack, because Minnesota’s is substantial. The state runs a paid family and medical leave program funded by payroll contributions, and has statewide earned sick and safe time requirements. Minneapolis and St. Paul have historically maintained their own minimum wage ordinances that differed from the state figure, so verify which applies at the specific business address and confirm the seller has been paying it. Also check worker classification carefully on anything staffed heavily with 1099 contractors — cleaning, landscaping, delivery, and home services are the usual suspects, and misclassification liability follows the business rather than the seller.

Confirm licenses genuinely transfer. Minnesota licenses residential building contractors at the state level, and plumbing, electrical, and HVAC work carries separate trade licensure held by individuals. Establish early whether you or a retained employee can hold the required license, because a trades business whose license walks out with the seller is a very different purchase. Liquor licensing runs through the municipality with its own transfer approval and timeline. Restaurants, childcare, and healthcare all carry state or county licensure. Pull the last two years of health and building inspection records on any physical-premises business.

Normalize for seasonality honestly — this is the Minneapolis-specific mistake. Landscaping, paving, roofing, exterior painting, and pressure washing compress most of their revenue into a short season here, and many pair with snow removal to survive the winter. When they do, you are underwriting two businesses with different equipment, different labor, and different risk, and snow revenue depends on weather that varies enormously year to year. Ask for three full years of monthly data, look at the trough rather than the average, and treat a single heavy snow year in the trailing twelve months as luck rather than run rate.

Read the lease before the financials. Remaining term, renewal options and who controls them, escalation schedule, assignment consent, and whether any personal guarantee survives. In a metro with meaningfully different occupancy costs between the downtowns and the suburban ring, the lease is frequently the largest single variable in whether the business works at your purchase price. Financing a Twin Cities acquisition

SBA 7(a) is the standard route for Main Street deals here and typically covers the large majority of the purchase price where the books are clean and cash flow covers debt service with room to spare. Minnesota has active SBA-preferred lenders alongside a notably strong community bank and credit union presence, and acquisition paper on a well-documented business is genuinely competitive. Recurring-revenue businesses underwrite best — commercial cleaning, pest control, property management, managed IT, and home care all have contracted, measurable revenue, and they are also the categories least exposed to the office-attendance questions that still make parts of the downtown consumer market harder to underwrite.

Where the seller owns the building — common in the suburban industrial corridors and among the trades and machining businesses — pairing an SBA 504 on the real estate with a 7(a) on the operating business is usually the better structure, because it converts occupancy cost into equity. Seller financing is worth pushing for on anything with customer concentration, heavy seasonality, or a downtown location; a held note or an earnout gives a confident seller a way to prove the revenue survives the handoff, and gives you protection if the snow season or the office traffic does not cooperate. Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Minneapolis?

BizBuySell carries the largest public inventory for the metro, and you should search the whole Twin Cities region rather than the city alone — a large share of listings sit in St. Paul and in the suburban ring including Bloomington, Eden Prairie, Minnetonka, Plymouth, Maple Grove, Woodbury, Eagan, and Burnsville. The metro also has a deep community of brokers, CPAs, and transaction attorneys, and many Main Street deals surface through them first. For businesses without a physical location, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere.

What kinds of businesses sell most often in the Twin Cities?

B2B and professional services supported by the region’s dense corporate headquarters base; healthcare and medical-adjacent practices; the building trades, where cold-climate housing stock keeps HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and roofing in structural demand; recurring-service businesses like commercial cleaning, landscaping with snow removal, and pest control; light manufacturing and machining across the suburban industrial corridors; and food and beverage in both downtowns and the suburbs.

What should out-of-state buyers know about Minnesota taxes and labor rules?

Minnesota carries a comparatively heavy compliance stack and it is where out-of-state buyers most often misjudge a deal. There is state income tax, a state sales and use tax with local add-ons that differ across the metro, a statewide paid leave program funded by payroll contributions, statewide earned sick and safe time, and city minimum wage ordinances that have differed from the state figure. Verify seller compliance with all of it during diligence, because unpaid payroll and sales tax liability commonly follows the business rather than the seller.

How does the winter affect evaluating a Minneapolis business?

More than buyers from milder climates expect. Landscaping, paving, roofing, exterior painting, and pressure washing compress revenue into a short season, and many pair with snow removal, which means you are underwriting two businesses with different equipment and labor — and snow revenue depends on weather that swings hard year to year. Restaurants with patios show a summer that flatters the annual average. Get three years of monthly data and underwrite the trough.

How do I finance a Minneapolis business acquisition?

Minnesota has active SBA-preferred lenders plus a strong community bank and credit union presence.
