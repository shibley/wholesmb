Empire Flippers Review: Is It Worth It for Buyers?

Empire Flippers sits at the opposite end of the marketplace spectrum from a classifieds site. It is a curated brokerage for online businesses — content sites, Amazon FBA brands, ecommerce stores and SaaS — where listings are vetted before they go live and a migration team helps move the asset after the deal closes.

That curation is the product, and it is also the trade-off. You see far fewer businesses than on an open marketplace, you generally have to clear a deposit or unlock step to view listing detail, and the prices reflect the fact that other pre-qualified buyers are looking at the same short list. This review is written from the buyer's side: what the vetting genuinely covers, what it does not, and who should be using a different marketplace instead.

What the vetting actually does

This is the part worth understanding properly, because it is the reason to be on the platform at all and the thing most reviews describe loosely. On a classifieds marketplace, a seller writes down what the business earns and the platform publishes it. Here, the seller is asked to grant access to the underlying accounts — analytics, ad networks, marketplace seller dashboards, payment processors — and the profit and loss is rebuilt from that data before the listing goes live.

The practical effect is that the obvious category of fabrication mostly disappears. You are far less likely to spend a week on a listing whose revenue simply never existed. That is a real saving, and on a portfolio of small online assets where the diligence cost per deal is high relative to the ticket, it matters.

What vetting does not do is underwrite the future. A site can have three verified years of traffic and still be one algorithm update or one supplier change away from a different business. An FBA brand can have clean numbers and a single hero product with a patent expiry or a competitor about to undercut it. Verified history is an input to your model, not a substitute for building one. The deposit and unlock model

Access to full listing detail has historically been gated. The intent is to keep tyre-kickers and competitors out of sellers' financials, which sellers reasonably care about. For a serious buyer it is a modest friction; for someone browsing casually to learn what online businesses cost, it is a wall, and an open marketplace is the better classroom.

Confirm the current terms on the site rather than relying on any article, including this one — marketplaces adjust deposit amounts, refund conditions and unlock mechanics from time to time, and the details determine whether the friction is trivial or meaningful for your budget.

Where the inventory is strong — and where it is thin

  • Content and affiliate sites. Well represented, with the traffic and earnings history you need to model them. The central risk is concentration in one search channel, which is a business risk rather than a marketplace failing.
  • Amazon FBA brands. A core category. Verify supplier terms, inventory position at close, and how much of the margin depends on a single SKU — see our FBA buying playbook.
  • Ecommerce stores. Available, and the questions are the familiar ones: repeat-purchase rate, paid-acquisition dependence, and whether the brand exists outside the ad account. Our ecommerce playbook goes through them.
  • SaaS. Present but a smaller slice, and the good listings move quickly. Churn cohorts matter more than headline monthly revenue — see buying a SaaS business.
  • Anything physical or local. Absent entirely. If you want a business with premises, staff and a lease, you want a Main Street listing site — start with our BizBuySell review.

Who should use it, and who should not

Use it if you are buying an established online business, you value a filtered pipeline over a large one, and you would rather pay for a managed migration than coordinate an account transfer yourself. The time saved on dead listings is the real return, and for buyers doing this alongside a job that time is the scarce resource.

Look elsewhere if you want maximum optionality and are comfortable verifying claims yourself — an open marketplace gives you far more shots on goal at the small end. Look elsewhere too if your budget is modest, since curated inventory clusters above the entry level, or if you want a local, physical business, which this marketplace simply does not sell.

So, is it worth using?

For its intended buyer, yes. The vetting removes a genuine category of wasted work, and the handover support is worth something on assets where a botched migration can cost real revenue in the first month. Just price the trade-off honestly: you are paying, in access friction and in valuation, for a shorter list that other qualified buyers are also looking at. That does not make it a bad deal, but it does mean your edge has to come from your underwriting rather than from finding something nobody else saw.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Empire Flippers free for buyers?

Browsing the marketplace is free once you create an account, but seeing the full listing detail on a business generally requires unlocking it, and the platform has historically used a refundable deposit to gate access to its inventory for serious buyers. The commission that funds the marketplace is paid by the seller out of the sale proceeds, so you are not paying a buyer-side success fee. Check the current deposit and unlock terms on the site before you plan around them, because marketplaces change these mechanics periodically.

How does Empire Flippers vetting work?

Before a business is listed, the marketplace asks the seller for access to the underlying accounts and analytics and reconstructs the profit and loss from that source data rather than from a spreadsheet the seller typed up. That is a meaningfully higher bar than a classifieds site, which publishes whatever the seller submits. What it is not is an audit or a guarantee. Vetting confirms the revenue and expense history the platform could observe at a point in time. It cannot tell you whether a traffic source is about to change, whether a supplier relationship is durable, or whether the niche is in structural decline. Your own diligence still decides the deal.

What kinds of businesses are listed on Empire Flippers?

Almost entirely online businesses: content and affiliate sites, Amazon FBA brands, ecommerce stores, SaaS products, and app or service businesses. There is no Main Street inventory at all, so if you want a laundromat, an HVAC company or a restaurant this is the wrong marketplace and you should be on a broker-led listing site instead. Within online businesses the inventory skews toward established assets with a trading history rather than brand-new projects.

Is Empire Flippers better than Flippa?

They are optimised for different things. Flippa is an open marketplace with far more listings across a much wider quality range, including very small and very speculative assets. Empire Flippers curates: fewer listings, a higher floor, verified financials, and a managed migration process. If you want maximum choice and are confident doing your own verification, the open marketplace gives you more shots. If you would rather pay a premium for a pre-filtered pipeline and a supported handover, the curated one saves you time. Neither removes the need to underwrite the business yourself.

Can you use an SBA loan to buy on Empire Flippers?

Sometimes, but it is harder than financing a Main Street business. SBA lenders can and do fund online-business acquisitions, and larger listings with several years of clean, verifiable financials and a defensible customer base are the realistic candidates. Small content sites, businesses dependent on a single traffic channel, and anything with a short operating history are usually declined. If financing is central to your plan, talk to a lender experienced in online acquisitions before you unlock listings, so you know what size and profile of deal you can actually close.
