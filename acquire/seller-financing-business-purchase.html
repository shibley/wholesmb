Seller Financing to Buy a Business

Seller financing — the seller accepts part of the purchase price as a promissory note paid over time instead of cash at closing — appears in a large share of small-business acquisitions, and for good reason. It closes the gap between what buyers can raise and what sellers want, and it does something no bank loan can: it keeps the person who knows the business financially exposed to how it performs after they hand over the keys. This guide covers what typical note terms look like, how seller paper interacts with SBA financing, how to raise the subject without losing the deal, and the protections that separate a good note from a bad one.

⚡ Quick Verdict

Push hard for a seller note when…

Value is concentrated in relationships or know-how the seller has to transfer, the business is goodwill-heavy, you need to bridge a gap between the price and your financing, or you want the seller motivated through the transition rather than gone at closing.

Be careful when…

The note is so large that debt service leaves no cushion, the seller demands a personal guarantee stacked on top of a bank's, there is no right of offset for misrepresentations, or the seller's eagerness to carry paper is really a signal that no cash buyer would touch the deal.

How a seller note is actually structured

Mechanically it's simple: at closing you pay part of the price in cash, and the balance becomes a promissory note payable to the seller, usually secured by the business assets and subordinated to any senior lender. On Main Street deals, notes commonly run 10–30% of the purchase price over three to seven years, with interest somewhere near or modestly above prevailing small-business lending rates. Nothing here is standardized — every term is negotiable, and the terms trade against each other. Sellers who care most about the headline price will usually concede on rate and term; sellers who care most about monthly cash flow will concede on price.

Two structural details matter more than the rate. The first is the amortization schedule: a note that fully amortizes over five years is very different from one with a balloon in year three, and the balloon is what tends to catch buyers who assumed they would refinance. The second is security and position. A seller in second position behind a bank has meaningfully less leverage than one holding the only lien, which is precisely why the seller will negotiate hard on it.

Combining a seller note with SBA financing

This is the workhorse structure of small-business acquisitions: an SBA 7(a) loan for the bulk of the price, a seller note for a slice, and buyer cash for the rest. The complication is standby. The SBA lender takes first position, and where a seller note is being counted toward your required equity injection, it generally must be on full standby — no principal or interest paid for a defined period at the start of the loan term. That is not a formality; it changes the seller's economics substantially and needs to be on the table before you sign an LOI, not discovered during underwriting.

Program specifics change, and individual lenders layer their own policy on top, so treat the standby terms as something to confirm with your specific SBA lender rather than assume. Our companion guide on buying a business with an SBA loan covers the injection rules and underwriting in more detail, and the low-cash structures guide covers how far the combination can actually be stretched.

Why sellers say yes

  • A bigger buyer pool. Requiring all cash eliminates most competent operators. Sellers who have already sat on the market for months learn this quickly.
  • A higher price. Carrying paper is routinely traded for the asking number. Buyers should note the flip side: a seller-financed deal is often a more expensive deal.
  • Tax timing. Installment payments can spread gain across years instead of concentrating it into one. This is a real motivator and worth naming in a negotiation.
  • Interest income. The note yields, often better than the alternatives for the proceeds.

It is also worth reading the refusal. A seller who will not carry any paper at all on a business they describe as stable is making a statement about their own confidence, and it is a fair question to ask directly.

What a buyer should negotiate into the note

The terms below are where buyer risk actually lives. The first one is the most important and the most frequently omitted.

👍 Clauses to fight for

  • Right of offset. If the seller's representations prove false, you reduce note payments instead of suing to claw money back. This is your single best post-closing protection.
  • A real cure period before a missed payment triggers default or reversion.
  • Prepayment without penalty, so a strong year lets you retire the note early.
  • Non-compete and a defined transition commitment tied to the note.
  • Clear subordination language agreed to by the senior lender up front.

👎 Terms to resist

  • A personal guarantee stacked on top of the one your bank already requires.
  • Reversion clauses that hand the business back to the seller on a single missed payment.
  • Balloon payments you have no documented plan to refinance.
  • A note so large that total debt service erases your operating cushion.
  • Payments that begin before you have collected a full cycle of receivables.

How to raise it without blowing up the deal

Bring it up in the letter of intent, not after diligence. Sellers who are surprised by a financing request late in a process usually read it as a renegotiation attempt, and they are often right. Frame it as alignment: a note gives the seller a continuing stake in a clean handoff, which is a genuine argument rather than a talking point. Arrive with a specific proposal — amount, term, rate, standby if SBA is involved — plus evidence that the business's cash flow services it comfortably. And be flexible on price in exchange, because that is the trade the seller is actually being offered. Whatever you agree, verify the earnings that will service the note yourself first; the due diligence checklist covers the tests that matter.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of the purchase price will a seller typically finance?

On Main Street deals, seller notes commonly cover somewhere between 10% and 30% of the purchase price, with terms in the three-to-seven-year range. Sellers who are retiring, who have already tried and failed to sell for cash, or who want the tax treatment of installment payments tend to go higher. The exact split is negotiable and usually moves with price: a seller who wants their asking number is far more likely to carry paper to get it.

Can you combine seller financing with an SBA loan?

Yes, and it is one of the most common structures in small-business acquisitions. The catch is subordination: the SBA lender will be in first position, and if the seller note is being counted toward your required equity injection it generally has to be on full standby, meaning no principal or interest payments for a defined period at the start of the loan term. Confirm the current standby requirements with your lender before you agree to note terms in an LOI.

Why would a seller agree to finance the sale of their own business?

Three reasons dominate. It widens the buyer pool, since most qualified operators do not have the full price in cash. It often gets the seller a higher headline price than an all-cash offer would. And installment payments can spread the tax hit across years rather than concentrating it in one. Carrying a note also signals confidence in the business, which is why buyers should treat a flat refusal to carry any paper as worth understanding.

What protections should a buyer negotiate into a seller note?

Push for a right of offset so that if the seller's representations turn out to be false you can reduce note payments rather than sue for the money back. Also negotiate a clear cure period before default, no personal guarantee beyond what the senior lender already requires if you can avoid it, prepayment without penalty, and a non-compete and transition commitment from the seller tied to the note. The offset right is the single most valuable clause and the one most often left out.

How do I ask a seller for financing without killing the deal?

Raise it early, in the letter of intent rather than after diligence, and frame it as alignment rather than as a discount. A note gives the seller ongoing financial interest in a clean handoff, which is a real argument, not a rhetorical one. Come with a specific structure, evidence that you can service the payments, and flexibility on price. Sellers rarely object to seller financing as a concept; they object to being surprised by it late in a negotiation.
