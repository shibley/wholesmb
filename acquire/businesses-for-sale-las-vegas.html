Businesses for Sale in Las Vegas: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Las Vegas is one of the friendliest states in the country to own a business in and one of the most procedurally demanding places to buy one. Nevada has no personal or corporate income tax, which is why so many owners relocate here, but it does tax gross revenue and payroll, and Clark County runs a licensing regime that is closer to a background investigation than a form. Add an economy where a large share of local spending traces back to tourism and convention traffic, and the two questions that decide most valley deals are: can you actually get the license, and how much of this revenue survives a slow tourism year? This guide covers where valley listings live, what sells, and how to run diligence that reflects those realities.

Where businesses for sale in Las Vegas are listed

BizBuySell is the primary aggregator for the valley, and you should search Clark County rather than the city of Las Vegas — Henderson, North Las Vegas, Summerlin, Spring Valley, Enterprise, and Paradise are distinct jurisdictions with their own licensing but one shared customer base, and a business that advertises as "Las Vegas" may be licensed somewhere else entirely. That distinction matters more here than in most metros, because your license application follows the jurisdiction, not the marketing. Nevada's broker community is active and clusters around hospitality, licensed, and route businesses, so a broker who has closed liquor or gaming-adjacent deals before is worth more than a generalist. For businesses that don't need a physical Nevada presence, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere.

Popular industries for sale in Las Vegas

The valley's listing mix reflects a hospitality economy, extreme desert heat, and a housing stock that is newer than almost any large metro in the country:

  • Food, beverage & taverns — restaurants, bars and taverns, and food trucks, the deepest category in the market and the one where licensing does the most to set price.
  • Heat-driven home services — HVAC, pool service, and landscaping, with HVAC the single most reliable trade to own in a market where summer failure is an emergency.
  • Personal care — salons, med spas, and barbershops, unusually dense here relative to population because of both the resident and visitor markets.
  • Retail & route businesses — liquor stores, convenience stores, and vending routes, several of which carry restricted gaming placements that materially change the economics.
  • Auto & storage — auto repair, car washes, towing, and self-storage, supported by a car-dependent valley and constant in-migration.
  • B2B services — commercial cleaning, property management, and facilities and linen services, much of which ultimately bills the hospitality sector.

Valley submarkets to watch

The Strip and Paradise corridor is tourism revenue in its purest form, with the highest ceilings and the sharpest downside in a slow year; treat any business there as a hospitality bet, not a Main Street one. Henderson skews affluent, residential, and stable, with the valley's strongest home-service and personal-care inventory and buyers who compete for it accordingly. Summerlin and the western suburbs are similar — newer housing, higher incomes, and service businesses that are growing but often too young to have real systems. North Las Vegas is the industrial and logistics side of the valley, where light-manufacturing, trade, and B2B service businesses trade at the best earnings-to-price ratios in the metro. The older central and east valley carries lower price points and a heavier concentration of convenience, liquor, and small-format retail, where the license attached to the business is frequently worth more than the fixtures. Confirm which jurisdiction issues the license before you assume a Henderson buyer can operate a North Las Vegas business without a fresh application.

How to evaluate a Las Vegas business listing

Start with licensing, and start early. Clark County and each incorporated city license businesses independently, and a broad set of categories — liquor, gaming, adult use, secondhand dealing, and several service trades — are treated as privileged licenses requiring individual suitability review of the applicant, including financial history and background investigation. That process takes real time, is measured in months rather than weeks for the more sensitive categories, and does not automatically follow the business to you. A tavern with a restricted gaming license, in particular, is not a normal asset purchase: the gaming portion involves the Nevada Gaming Control Board, and a buyer who assumes those revenues transfer on closing day can end up owning a bar with a dark corner where the profit used to be. Never sign a purchase agreement in this market without a licensing contingency that lets you walk with your deposit if approval doesn't come through.

Second, separate tourist revenue from local revenue. Ask for revenue by day of week and by month across at least three years, and look for the fingerprint of convention calendars and event weekends. A restaurant, salon, or transport business that looks strong on averages may in reality be earning most of its money on a handful of peak weeks, which is both a concentration risk and a staffing problem. Businesses serving Henderson and Summerlin residents behave much more like a normal suburban market, and the multiple you pay should reflect which of the two you are actually buying.

Third, get the tax picture right. Nevada's lack of income tax does not mean no state tax: the Commerce Tax applies to Nevada gross revenue above a statutory threshold, with rates that vary by industry classification, and the Modified Business Tax is levied on wages. Both are structurally different from an income tax, so a P&L from a Nevada seller cannot be compared line-for-line to one from Phoenix or California without adjustment — have a CPA confirm current thresholds, rates, and classification before you model the deal. Then verify that any commercial lease survives the sale, since landlord consent is a routine deal-killer in the valley's strip-center retail. Financing a Las Vegas acquisition

SBA 7(a) loans remain the standard acquisition route and typically cover 70–90% of the purchase price where financials are clean and documented. Two Las Vegas-specific wrinkles matter. First, SBA lenders will not finance a business whose revenue depends materially on gaming, which puts taverns with restricted gaming and some route operators outside conventional SBA reach — those deals typically get done with seller financing or private capital, which is one reason sellers in those categories are more flexible on terms than the asking price suggests. Second, underwriters in this market discount tourism-dependent revenue and will want to see the local-resident share of the customer base, so a business with contracted B2B or recurring residential revenue underwrites far more easily than a Strip-adjacent consumer business with identical earnings. Where the deal includes real estate, an SBA 504 alongside the 7(a) is usually the better structure, and valley industrial and flex space remains attainable for owner-operators. Seller financing is common and worth negotiating hard for here, particularly where a licensing contingency means the seller needs you to get through approval as much as you do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Las Vegas?

BizBuySell has the deepest valley coverage — search Clark County rather than the city, since Henderson, North Las Vegas, Summerlin, and Paradise are separate licensing jurisdictions sharing one customer base. For licensed categories, use a broker who has closed liquor or gaming-adjacent deals before. For remote-run businesses, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are most commonly for sale in Las Vegas?

Restaurants, bars, and taverns lead, followed by heat-driven home services like HVAC, pool service, and landscaping; salons and med spas; liquor and convenience stores and vending routes; auto repair, towing, and self-storage; and B2B services that ultimately bill the hospitality sector.

How much do businesses for sale in Las Vegas typically cost?

Most Main Street listings fall between roughly $100,000 and $800,000, with established trade and licensed hospitality businesses running higher. Pricing generally runs 2x–4x SDE, and a business holding a hard-to-obtain license — a tavern with restricted gaming, or liquor in a capped jurisdiction — often carries a premium beyond what its earnings alone support.

What should I watch out for when buying a business in Las Vegas?

Licensing first: privileged licenses require individual suitability review, take months, and do not automatically transfer — always include a licensing contingency. Then separate tourist revenue from local revenue using day-of-week and month-by-month data, confirm Commerce Tax and Modified Business Tax treatment with a CPA, and get landlord consent to the lease assignment in writing.
