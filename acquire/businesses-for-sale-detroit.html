Businesses for Sale in Detroit, MI: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Metro Detroit is one of the most interesting acquisition markets in the country and one of the easiest to misprice. The interesting part is a genuine industrial base — machine shops, tool and die, contract manufacturers, coaters, and industrial service firms — owned in large numbers by people at retirement age, with real equipment, real skilled labor, and asking multiples well below what comparable revenue commands in a coastal metro. The mispricing part is that a lot of those same businesses depend on a handful of automotive customers and on programs with end dates, and that Michigan layers a city income tax onto some addresses in the metro and not others. This guide covers where tri-county listings live, which categories trade, what to verify, and how these deals get financed.

Where businesses for sale in Detroit are listed

BizBuySell carries the largest public inventory for the metro, but a search restricted to the city of Detroit will miss most of it. This is a heavily suburban, tri-county region and the listing volume reflects that. Oakland County — Troy, Southfield, Royal Oak, Birmingham, Rochester Hills, Novi, Farmington Hills, Auburn Hills — holds a large share of the professional-service, B2B, healthcare, and higher-priced consumer inventory. Macomb County — Sterling Heights, Warren, Clinton Township, Shelby Township — is where much of the machining, fabrication, and industrial-service inventory sits, along with a deep bench of trades and consumer-service businesses at more accessible price points. Wayne County outside the city, including Livonia, Canton, Dearborn, and the Downriver communities, covers both.

The metro also has an unusually deep intermediary community, built up over decades around the supply base. A real share of industrial and B2B deals here move through local business brokers, manufacturing-focused CPAs, and transaction attorneys before they ever reach a public portal — and in tooling, machining, and contract manufacturing that share is higher still. If you know the category you want, telling three or four local accountants and a couple of brokers exactly what you are looking for is a genuinely productive use of a week. For businesses that do not need a physical footprint, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere in the metro.

Popular industries for sale in Metro Detroit

The listing mix reflects an industrial supply base, an older housing stock, a hard winter, and a wealthy suburban ring:

  • Machining, tooling, and contract manufacturing — machine shops, tool and die, fabrication, and coating. The category that defines this market and the one first-time buyers both overlook and underestimate the risk in. Real equipment, real skilled labor, and the concentration problem covered below.
  • Industrial and commercial services — equipment repair, industrial cleaning, freight and trucking, and moving companies, all supported by the manufacturing and logistics base.
  • Building trades — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and roofing. One of the oldest housing stocks of any large metro plus a genuinely cold winter makes heating, envelope, and ice-related work recurring rather than discretionary.
  • Recurring-service businesses — commercial cleaning, landscaping paired with snow removal, pest control, and property management.
  • Healthcare and practices — dental, veterinary, and chiropractic practices, plus home care agencies, concentrated in the Oakland County suburbs.
  • Consumer and food service — restaurants, bars, coffee shops, salons, gyms, car washes, and auto repair, which is unusually dense here for obvious reasons.

Metro Detroit submarkets to watch

The city of Detroit — downtown, Midtown, Corktown, and the New Center have seen sustained reinvestment, and there is real consumer-business inventory there now that did not exist fifteen years ago. But the city is also where the tax and compliance layer is heaviest, where property tax assessments and any outstanding municipal obligations on a building deserve a title search rather than a seller assurance, and where block-level differences in foot traffic are larger than in any suburban submarket. Walk the specific block at the specific hours the business earns its money before you underwrite it.

Oakland County carries the metro’s highest household incomes and the densest concentration of corporate and professional offices. This is where B2B services, professional practices, healthcare, and trades with commercial client bases sit, and where asking prices are highest. It is also where a seller is most likely to own the building, which opens the door to an SBA 504 structure on the real estate.

Macomb County is the industrial heart of the listing market. The Sterling Heights and Warren corridors hold much of the machining and fabrication inventory, generally at lower multiples than an equivalent shop in a metro without a concentrated customer base — which is the market pricing the concentration risk, not a bargain nobody noticed.

Watch the municipal income tax boundary. Detroit levies a city income tax, and so do a number of other Michigan municipalities including Highland Park, Hamtramck, Pontiac, and Flint, while most Oakland and Macomb suburbs do not. Two otherwise identical businesses a few miles apart can therefore have different withholding obligations and different effective compensation costs. Confirm the rule for the exact address rather than assuming a metro-wide answer, and check how it applies to employees who work at customer sites in other jurisdictions.

How to evaluate a Detroit business listing

Start with customer and program concentration — this is the Detroit-specific mistake. A large share of the metro’s most attractive-looking industrial listings derive most of their revenue from a small number of automakers or Tier 1 suppliers, frequently on work tied to specific vehicle platforms with defined end dates. Ask for revenue by customer and by program for three full years. Find out when each current program ends, whether the business has been awarded follow-on work, and whether the purchase orders are firm contracts or blanket releases the customer can reduce. A shop with excellent trailing earnings and no awarded program past next year is a fundamentally different asset from one with three years of booked work, and the multiple should reflect that.

Verify revenue against sales tax filings where the business is taxable. Michigan levies a state sales and use tax, and for any retail, restaurant, or taxable-service business the filed state returns are a revenue floor the seller had every incentive not to overstate. Reconcile them against bank deposits, merchant processor statements, and filed federal returns. Unpaid sales and payroll tax liability commonly follows the business rather than the seller, so a clean-standing letter from the state is worth insisting on before closing.

Confirm licenses genuinely transfer. Michigan licenses residential builders and maintenance and alteration contractors at the state level, and the mechanical, plumbing, and electrical trades carry separate licensure held by individuals. Establish early whether you or a retained employee can hold the required license, because a trades business whose license leaves with the seller is a very different purchase. Liquor licensing runs through the state liquor control commission with local approval, its own transfer process, and a timeline that can outlast a purchase agreement’s financing contingency. Restaurants, childcare, and healthcare all carry state or county licensure — pull the last two years of health and building inspection records on any physical-premises business.

Underwrite the winter and the equipment together. Landscaping, paving, roofing, exterior painting, and pressure washing compress most of their revenue into a short season here, and many pair with snow removal to fill the rest of the year, which means you are buying two businesses with different equipment, labor, and risk. Snow revenue also swings hard with weather, so a single heavy season in the trailing twelve months is luck, not run rate. On the industrial side, get an independent condition assessment of the machines: a shop’s reported earnings can be flattered by years of deferred capital spending, and CNC replacement is not a small number.

Read the lease before the financials. Remaining term, renewal options and who controls them, escalation schedule, assignment consent, and whether any personal guarantee survives. Financing a Metro Detroit acquisition

SBA 7(a) is the standard route for Main Street and lower-middle-market deals here and typically covers the large majority of the purchase price where the books are clean and cash flow covers debt service with room to spare. Michigan has active SBA-preferred lenders alongside a strong community bank and credit union presence, and lenders in this market understand manufacturing collateral better than lenders in most metros do. What they will underwrite hardest is exactly the thing you should be underwriting hardest: customer concentration. Expect a lender to ask for the same program-by-program revenue detail you should already have requested, and treat a lender’s discomfort with a shop’s concentration as information rather than an obstacle.

Where the seller owns the building — extremely common in the Macomb and Oakland industrial corridors — pairing an SBA 504 on the real estate with a 7(a) on the operating business is usually the better structure, because it converts occupancy cost into equity. And on anything with automotive concentration, push hard for seller financing or an earnout: a seller who genuinely believes the programs will be re-awarded should be willing to carry part of that risk, and a seller who is not willing has told you something useful. Buying with little money down covers the structures buyers ask about most.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Detroit?

BizBuySell carries the largest public inventory for Metro Detroit, and you should search the tri-county region rather than the city alone — the majority of listings sit in Oakland and Macomb counties, including Troy, Southfield, Royal Oak, Rochester Hills, Novi, Farmington Hills, Sterling Heights, Warren, and Clinton Township. The metro also has a deep intermediary community built around the automotive supply base, and many industrial and B2B deals surface through brokers, CPAs, and transaction attorneys first. For businesses without a physical location, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere.

What kinds of businesses sell most often in Metro Detroit?

Machining, tooling, and contract manufacturing tied to the automotive supply chain; industrial and commercial services such as equipment repair, logistics, and coatings; the building trades, where an old housing stock and hard winters keep HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and roofing in structural demand; recurring-service businesses like commercial cleaning, landscaping with snow removal, and pest control; healthcare practices across the Oakland County suburbs; and food, beverage, and auto repair throughout the metro.

What is the biggest diligence risk when buying a Detroit business?

Customer and program concentration. Many of the metro’s best-looking industrial listings earn most of their revenue from a few automakers or Tier 1 suppliers, on work tied to specific vehicle platforms with end dates. Ask for revenue by customer and by program across three years, establish when each program ends and whether follow-on work has been awarded, and confirm whether the purchase orders are firm contracts or blanket releases. Strong trailing earnings with no awarded program past next year is not worth the same multiple.

Does Detroit have a city income tax that affects a business purchase?

Yes. Detroit levies a municipal income tax, as do several other Michigan cities including Highland Park, Hamtramck, Pontiac, and Flint, while most suburban Oakland and Macomb communities do not. Withholding obligations, effective employee take-home pay, and in some cases the business’s own filing obligations therefore differ by street address inside the same metro. Confirm the rule for the specific location and for employees working elsewhere, and verify the seller’s filings are current — delinquent withholding is among the liabilities most likely to follow the business.

How do I finance a Detroit business acquisition?

Michigan has active SBA-preferred lenders plus a strong community bank and credit union presence, and local lenders understand manufacturing collateral well. On anything with automotive customer concentration, push hard for seller financing or an earnout.
