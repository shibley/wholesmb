Businesses for Sale in North Dakota: Buyer's Guide 2026

North Dakota pairs one of the lowest state income tax burdens in the country with a genuinely diversified Fargo economy spanning technology, healthcare, and financial services, while Bismarck serves as the state's government and commercial hub. The Bakken oil-patch region in the west has driven boom-and-bust energy cycles for over a decade, and the rest of the state's economy leans on wheat, soybean, and canola farming and the trades that support it. This guide covers where to search, what industries dominate around Fargo, Bismarck, and the Bakken region, what deals cost, and the state-specific quirks that shape a North Dakota acquisition.

What to expect on price

Established Fargo and Bismarck businesses typically sell at 2.5x–3.5x SDE, roughly in line with the national average, with North Dakota's low tax burden improving after-tax buyer economics relative to that multiple. Bakken-region energy-services businesses trade at a wider range and often a discount, reflecting the sector's history of sharp commodity-driven swings.

Why buyers target North Dakota

North Dakota's pitch to business buyers starts with tax and cost structure: one of the lowest state income tax burdens in the country, low property and business costs, and a Fargo economy that has diversified well beyond agriculture into technology, healthcare, and financial services — several national companies run significant back-office and processing operations there. For a buyer financing a deal with an SBA loan, that combination means more of the business's cash flow is available to service debt and reinvest, rather than disappearing into state tax.

Regionally, Fargo is the state's largest and most diversified economy, anchored by North Dakota State University, healthcare systems, and a growing technology sector. Bismarck serves as the state capital and a regional commercial and trade hub. The Bakken oil-patch region in the state's northwest, centered on Williston and Watford City, has driven a boom-and-bust energy cycle since the mid-2000s shale boom, supporting oilfield services, trucking, housing, and hospitality businesses that expand and contract with oil prices. The rest of the state is largely agricultural, growing wheat, soybeans, and canola, supporting a steady base of trades, equipment, and grain-handling businesses. Buyers should model North Dakota's very small overall population and buyer pool outside Fargo and Bismarck, and build real commodity-price cyclicality into projections for any Bakken-region business.

Most common business types for sale in North Dakota

North Dakota's business-for-sale market splits between Fargo's diversified commercial economy, Bakken-region energy services, and a broad agricultural base:

  • Technology and business-services firms — software, back-office, and professional-services businesses clustered around Fargo's diversified economy.
  • Energy and oilfield-services businesses — trucking, equipment rental, and services companies tied to the Bakken shale region around Williston and Watford City.
  • Agricultural and agribusiness-adjacent companies — equipment dealers, grain-handling, and farm-supply businesses tied to the state's wheat, soybean, and canola economy.
  • Trades businesses — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and general contracting, in steady demand statewide as owners retire.
  • Healthcare-adjacent practices — dental, veterinary, and home care agencies serving both metro and rural populations.
  • Trucking and logistics businesses — regional freight tied to both agricultural shipping and Bakken-region energy transport.

Valuation: what North Dakota businesses sell for

Pricing follows the same general framework as the rest of the U.S. — a multiple of seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) — with established Fargo and Bismarck businesses commonly selling at 2.5x–3.5x SDE, roughly in line with the national average. North Dakota's low tax burden and low operating costs mean a buyer's after-tax cash flow at a given multiple tends to run ahead of what the same multiple would generate in a higher-tax state. Bakken-region energy-services businesses are the exception: buyers and lenders apply a wider range of multiples and often a discount to account for the sector's history of sharp booms and busts tied to oil prices, so sellers who can document performance through a full commodity cycle command better terms.

Always normalize the financials yourself, and for any Bakken-region business, request several years of revenue history spanning at least one full oil-price cycle — a single boom or bust year can badly distort a single annual figure.

Financing a North Dakota business purchase

SBA 7(a) loans remain the most common financing path for established, profitable businesses. North Dakota's community banks and credit unions, particularly around Fargo and Bismarck, are active in acquisition lending. Bakken-region energy-services businesses can be harder to finance through conventional channels since lenders want to see the business can service debt through a commodity-price downturn — sellers of these businesses are more likely to offer seller financing or require a larger down payment to bridge that gap. You'll typically need roughly 10% down, solid personal credit, and at least two years of verifiable earnings for a standard SBA deal.

North Dakota-specific due diligence checklist

Standard due diligence applies everywhere, but North Dakota has a few state-specific wrinkles worth flagging early:

  • Oil-price cyclicality — for any Bakken-region business, verify revenue and staffing history through at least one full commodity-price cycle, not just recent boom years.
  • Mineral rights — for businesses with agricultural or rural real estate in western North Dakota, confirm whether mineral rights are included, severed, or leased, since surface and mineral ownership are frequently split.
  • Agricultural cyclicality — for businesses serving farmers, factor in commodity-price cycles for wheat, soybeans, and canola, plus weather-driven swings in the local customer base's spending power.
  • Workforce availability — Bakken-region businesses have historically faced acute labor shortages during boom periods; confirm current staffing stability and wage levels before assuming past margins hold.
  • Housing and transient population — in Bakken-region towns, verify whether the customer base includes a significant transient oilfield workforce, which can inflate or deflate revenue depending on the current drilling cycle.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many businesses are for sale in North Dakota?

BizBuySell lists a small but steady inventory of North Dakota businesses, concentrated around Fargo, Bismarck, and the Bakken oil-patch region in the western part of the state.

What types of businesses sell most often in North Dakota?

Energy and oilfield-services businesses in the Bakken region, agricultural companies tied to wheat, soybean, and canola farming, trades and construction businesses in Fargo, and Main Street retail in regional hub towns are the most commonly listed categories.

Is North Dakota a good state to buy a small business?

North Dakota has one of the lowest state income tax burdens in the country and a diversified Fargo economy. Trade-offs include a very small overall buyer pool and real cyclicality tied to energy and commodity prices.

Can I use an SBA loan to buy a North Dakota business?

Yes. SBA 7(a) loans are available statewide, and North Dakota's community banks and credit unions, particularly around Fargo and Bismarck, are active in acquisition lending.
