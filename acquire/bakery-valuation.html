How Much Is a Bakery Worth?

Bakeries are one of the few categories where two businesses with identical revenue and identical profit are worth genuinely different amounts of money, and the reason is the sales channel. A retail counter bakery sells to whoever walks past, at a margin that funds a lot of labor, with an owner who is usually the baker. A wholesale bakery sells contracted volume to grocers and restaurants on written terms with a production team that shows up whether the owner does or not. The second one is a business; the first one is often a job with an oven. This page covers the multiple ranges each actually trades in, what moves a bakery up or down its range, the owner-baker problem that reprices most deals, and a worked example.

⚡ The Short Answer

Typical range

1.5x–2.5x SDE for a retail counter bakery, with 2x a fair starting point before adjustments. 2.5x–3.5x SDE, or a 4x-plus adjusted EBITDA basis, for a wholesale bakery with contracted accounts and a production team. Very small bakeries can price closer to the appraised value of the equipment than to any multiple of earnings.

Priced on

SDE wherever the owner bakes, opens, or works the counter; adjusted EBITDA once the bakery can carry a head baker and a manager at market wage. Ovens, mixers, and display cases are not added on top — their age shows up as an adjustment. Owned real estate is valued separately.

How bakeries are priced

Normalize the earnings, then apply a multiple that reflects risk — and in this category the normalization does most of the work. Start by paying every role at market. An owner who bakes the morning production is doing skilled work that a replacement earns a real wage for, and if that is not expensed, the reported SDE is overstated by the entire cost of a baker. Do the same for a spouse working the counter unpaid and for any family labor. Then add a genuine equipment reserve: ovens, proofers, mixers, and refrigeration wear out, and a bakery that has deferred all of it is showing you earnings it borrowed from your first two years.

The general mechanics — what qualifies as an add-back, how SDE differs from EBITDA, how working capital is handled at close — are covered in how to value a business. Below is what is specific to bakeries.

What moves the multiple

  • Channel mix — The dominant driver. Get revenue split across retail counter, wholesale accounts, custom and special-order cakes, and any cafe or seating revenue, for the trailing three years. Contracted wholesale volume supports a higher multiple than walk-in traffic because it is forecastable and it lets the buyer plan production instead of guessing.
  • Whether the head baker stays — A tenured baker who is not the seller and who commits to stay post-close is worth real multiple. It converts the business from a personal craft into a transferable operation.
  • Recipe and process documentation — Written, scaled, costed formulas and a documented production schedule. Undocumented recipes held in the seller's head are a transfer risk that belongs in the price and in the closing conditions.
  • Wholesale account quality — Several mid-sized accounts on written terms beats one anchor grocery relationship at the same revenue. Ask for account-level revenue, tenure, and whether agreements survive a change of ownership.
  • Waste and food cost discipline — Waste as a percentage of production, and food cost as a percentage of sales, tracked over time. Tight numbers signal a real operating system; high or untracked waste signals either poor planning or earnings that will not reconcile.
  • Custom cake and event revenue — High margin and often booked weeks ahead, but frequently tied to one decorator's skill and portfolio. Valuable if the decorator stays and the order book is in a system; fragile if it lives in a personal Instagram account.
  • Lease, kitchen buildout, and hood — A long lease at a defensible rent in a location with morning foot traffic is a large part of a retail bakery's value. The buildout matters too: a compliant hood, fire suppression, and adequate three-phase power are expensive to add and cheap to inherit.

What pulls the price down

These are the findings that most often reprice a bakery deal between the letter of intent and the closing table. Each is a reason to bid below the mid-range, or to move part of the price into a seller note or an earnout.

  • The seller is the head baker, with no replacement identified and no post-close training period committed in writing.
  • Recipes, production schedules, and supplier specifications that exist only as the owner's habit.
  • A single wholesale account producing a large share of revenue, especially a grocery chain that re-bids annually.
  • A short remaining lease, an above-market rent, or a landlord who has not confirmed they will assign to a buyer.
  • Reported cash sales that do not reconcile to point-of-sale records, deposits, and ingredient purchases — an unusually common issue in this category.
  • Deferred equipment maintenance, an aging oven, or refrigeration and hood systems that would not pass a current inspection.
  • Revenue growth driven entirely by delivery apps at commission rates that leave little or no margin on the incremental order.

Worked example: a $145,000 SDE retail bakery

A neighborhood retail bakery reports $620,000 of revenue and $145,000 of SDE. The owner bakes six mornings a week and their spouse works the counter unpaid; a replacement baker and a part-time counter employee cost roughly $72,000 fully loaded. Normalized earnings are therefore nearer to $73,000. At a mid-range 2x, that is about $146,000. Now adjust: roughly 30% of revenue comes from four wholesale cafe accounts averaging four years of tenure with written terms (up), the lease has six years remaining at a reasonable rent with an assignment clause the landlord has confirmed (up), but the primary deck oven is fourteen years old with a realistic $35,000 replacement inside two years (down), recipes are undocumented (down, and a closing condition rather than only a price issue), and the seller will not commit past a two-week handover (down). A realistic bid lands near $120,000–$150,000, with a portion carried as a seller note, the oven replacement negotiated as a price reduction, and a written recipe handover plus a ninety-day training commitment made conditions of closing.

Run the same arithmetic on any listing. Restate the earnings with a real baker's wage and every family hour paid at market, subtract an annual equipment reserve, divide the asking price by what remains, and then ask what in this specific bakery justifies its position in the range. If the honest answer is that the pastries are excellent, remember that you are being asked to pay for the seller's hands, and those are not included in the sale.

Before you rely on any of this

Market ranges orient a first conversation; they do not price a deal. Once past the initial screen, get three years of tax returns and reconcile them to point-of-sale exports and bank deposits rather than to the broker's summary — you want daily sales by category, transaction counts, wholesale revenue by account, and ingredient purchases from the distributor's own statements. Cash-heavy retail food businesses require more reconciliation than most categories, and unverifiable cash sales are worth nothing in a valuation no matter how confidently they are described. Also confirm the health inspection history and that the hood and suppression systems are current. Frequently Asked Questions

What multiple do bakeries sell for?

Retail bakeries commonly trade at roughly 1.5x–2.5x SDE, which is at the low end of the small-business range because the category is labor-intensive, perishable, and usually dependent on a single owner-baker. Wholesale bakeries with contracted accounts and a real production team price higher, often 2.5x–3.5x SDE or a 4x-plus adjusted EBITDA basis, because their revenue is contracted, forecastable, and does not depend on foot traffic past one storefront.

Does the bakery equipment add to the price?

Not on top of a cash-flow multiple — the ovens, mixers, and cases are what produce the earnings you are already paying for. Equipment shows up as an adjustment. A deck or rack oven can run $15,000–$60,000 or more installed, and a hood and fire-suppression system is its own significant cost, so an aging kitchen is a near-term capital call that belongs in your bid. On very small bakeries the appraised equipment value can exceed a couple of years of earnings, and the deal then prices closer to assets than to cash flow.

How much does the owner-baker problem lower the value?

Substantially, and it is the single most common reason a bakery deal reprices. If the seller is the head baker, the earnings include unpaid skilled labor that starts at 3am, and a replacement baker at market wage often consumes a large share of reported SDE. Beyond the wage, recipes and technique that live only in the seller's head are a transfer risk. Buyers respond by expensing a real baker's salary, lowering the multiple, and requiring a documented recipe handover and a training period as closing conditions.

Are wholesale accounts worth more than retail counter sales?

Per dollar of earnings, generally yes, because contracted grocery, cafe, restaurant, and institutional accounts produce forecastable volume that supports efficient production planning and lower waste. The offset is concentration and margin: wholesale gross margins are thinner than counter retail, and losing one large grocery account can remove a quarter of the revenue overnight. The valuable profile is a bakery with several mid-sized accounts on written terms, not one anchored to a single buyer.

How is spoilage and waste treated in a bakery valuation?

As a quality-of-earnings question rather than an add-back. Bakery product is perishable within a day or two, so waste percentage is a direct read on how well production is matched to demand. Ask for waste tracked as a share of production for the trailing year. A bakery reporting strong SDE alongside high unexplained waste is either underreporting cash sales or running production it does not need, and both are reasons to discount the multiple until the numbers reconcile.
