How to Buy a Machine Shop: The Complete Playbook

A machine shop is one of the few Main Street acquisitions where the hard assets can be worth as much as the business. That single fact changes how you underwrite it. A CNC job shop is a capacity business — you are buying machine hours, the skill to quote them profitably, and a customer list that buys them on purchase orders rather than contracts. The upside is a financeable asset base, real barriers to entry, and a seller cohort reaching retirement with few internal successors. The risks are concentrated and specific: one customer that is half the revenue, an owner who is also the estimator, and a machinist labor market that has been tight for a decade. This playbook covers what to pay, what to inspect, and where these deals actually go wrong.

⚡ Quick Verdict

Buy a machine shop if…

The equipment appraises near or above the asking price, revenue is spread across a reasonable number of customers, the estimating and floor-supervision functions sit with employees who are staying, certifications are current and transferable, and you can fund working capital on top of the purchase price.

Think twice if…

One customer is most of the revenue, the owner personally quotes every job and holds every relationship, the machines are old enough that a large capital replacement is due, the shop is running near capacity with no room to grow, or you have no answer for who runs the floor on day one.

How a machine shop actually makes money

A job shop sells machine hours with material and skill wrapped around them. Revenue is the product of available spindle hours, how many of those hours are billable, and the effective rate the shop achieves on them. Profit is what survives after material, labor, tooling, and the overhead of keeping expensive machines and expensive people ready for work that arrives unevenly.

That structure has three consequences a buyer should internalize. First, quoting is the profit engine. A job priced on optimistic cycle times consumes hours you cannot recover, and by the time it shows up in the annual financials the damage is done. Shops that price well are usually shops where one experienced person quotes carefully — and that person is frequently the owner who is leaving. Second, utilization drives everything. A shop at eighty percent capacity has limited organic upside without capital or a second shift; a shop at forty percent has room but you should understand why the room exists. Third, setup time is the hidden variable. Short-run, high-mix work carries far more setup per billable hour than long production runs, so two shops with identical revenue can have very different margins depending on their job mix.

The better shops move up this ladder over time: from simple prints and low-mix parts, to tight-tolerance and multi-axis work, to full-service programs with inspection, assembly, finishing, and managed inventory. Each step raises the rate a customer will pay and makes the shop harder to replace.

What does it cost to buy a machine shop?

Pricing spans a wide range because equipment is a large part of the value:

  • Small owner-operated shops — two or three machines, the owner on the floor, revenue concentrated in a handful of local customers. These trade in the low-to-mid six figures, and the price often tracks the equipment value closely.
  • Established multi-machine shops — several CNC machining centers and lathes, a dedicated inspection function, a foreman below the owner, and a diversified customer base. These move into the seven-figure range and are priced on an earnings multiple with the equipment supporting the debt.
  • Certified and specialty shops — AS9100 aerospace, medical device work, multi-axis or Swiss capability. These command premium multiples because the certification and qualification history are genuine barriers to entry that a new entrant cannot replicate quickly.

The most valuable single test in this category is to commission an independent equipment appraisal — specifically an orderly-liquidation value, not a replacement-cost number — and compare it to the asking price. If the earnings-based value sits below the appraised equipment value, your downside is unusually well protected. If the asking price is far above it, you are paying for goodwill that lives in relationships and knowledge, and you need to satisfy yourself that both transfer. Price the deal with our business valuation guide and calculator, and be honest about which earnings year you are applying the multiple to, because manufacturing demand is cyclical.

Budget working capital separately and generously. Job shops carry raw material, work in process, and receivables from customers who often pay on extended terms, while payroll and material bills arrive on time. Undercapitalized working capital is one of the most common ways an otherwise sound acquisition gets into trouble in the first year.

Financing a machine shop acquisition

  • SBA 7(a) — the standard route for most Main Street machine shop deals, and lenders are generally comfortable here precisely because there is collateral behind the loan. Our SBA acquisition-loan guide covers the process and what underwriting looks for.
  • SBA 504 on the building — machine shops are frequently owner-occupied industrial property, and separating the real estate into a 504 while the operating business goes on a 7(a) usually produces better terms on both.
  • Equipment financing — because CNC machinery holds resale value and has an active secondary market, it can be financed or refinanced on its own. This is also how you fund post-close capacity additions without touching the acquisition loan.
  • Seller financing — particularly appropriate where customer concentration is high or the owner has been the estimator. A held note or earnout aligns the seller with a transition that actually holds; see our seller financing guide.

What to inspect before you buy

  • Revenue by customer, three years — the concentration number is the headline risk in this category. Find out who the top customers are, what share each represents, whether the relationship belongs to the owner or to a staying employee, and whether the work runs on purchase orders or on anything resembling a contract. Most job shop revenue is PO-based and therefore not committed to you after closing.
  • Approved-supplier status — many industrial and aerospace customers maintain qualified-supplier lists, and a change of ownership can trigger re-qualification. Ask directly whether that is the case for the largest accounts, and get the answer from the customer, not only from the seller.
  • Equipment list with age, hours, and service history — machine by machine. Ask what has been rebuilt, what is nearing end of life, what is under maintenance contract, and what the last three years of capital spending looked like. Deferred maintenance and deferred replacement are both real liabilities that do not appear on the balance sheet.
  • An independent equipment appraisal — do not rely on the seller's depreciated book value or on a replacement-cost figure. Orderly liquidation value is the number that tells you what your collateral is actually worth.
  • Capacity and utilization — how many spindle hours exist, how many are billed, how many shifts run, and where the bottleneck sits. This tells you whether growth requires capital, people, or just more work.
  • Quoting and job costing records — quoted versus actual hours by job, if the shop tracks it. A shop that measures this is a better-run shop, and the data tells you immediately whether estimates are systematically optimistic.
  • Certifications and their audit calendar — ISO 9001, AS9100 for aerospace, IATF 16949 for automotive, plus ITAR registration where defense-related technical data is involved and any cybersecurity obligations attached to government work. Verify each is current, learn when the next surveillance audit falls, and confirm transferability on a change of control.
  • People — machinists, programmers, the foreman, and the estimator. This is a persistently tight labor market, and a shop that loses two experienced machinists loses capacity it cannot quickly rebuy. Understand tenure, pay relative to the local market, and who is genuinely load-bearing. Talk about retention packages before closing rather than after.
  • Environmental and safety — coolant, oils, solvents, chip and swarf handling, waste disposal records, air permits where applicable, and OSHA history including machine guarding and lockout-tagout. Where you are acquiring real estate, a Phase I environmental assessment is standard and worth doing properly.
  • Quality history — scrap and rework rates, customer corrective action requests, and on-time delivery performance. Poor delivery performance is often the real reason a customer is quietly moving work elsewhere, and it will not be volunteered.
  • Backlog and open orders — what is actually booked at closing, and how that compares to the same point in prior years. A thin backlog behind a strong trailing year deserves an explanation.

Run all of this alongside the general business due diligence checklist, and follow the overall process in how to buy a business.

Pros and cons

👍 Pros

  • Hard assets with an active secondary market back a large share of the price.
  • Real barriers to entry — capital, skill, and customer qualification all take years to replicate.
  • Certifications and approved-supplier status are durable competitive moats.
  • B2B customers, business-hours operation, and no consumer foot traffic to defend.
  • A large cohort of retiring owner-operators with few internal successors.
  • Clear growth levers: a second shift, better quoting, and moving up the capability ladder.

👎 Cons

  • Customer concentration is the norm, and PO-based work carries no commitment to a new owner.
  • Skilled machinists and programmers are genuinely hard to hire and replace.
  • Quoting skill often sits with the departing owner.
  • Demand is cyclical and tracks industrial capital spending.
  • Capital equipment replacement is expensive and easy to defer past the point of safety.
  • Working capital requirements are heavy — material and receivables both tie up cash.
  • Environmental, safety, and export-control compliance add real overhead.

Ready to look at listings?

Machine shops appear regularly on the general business-for-sale marketplaces, usually under manufacturing categories, and they also trade through industrial-focused brokers and directly between shops in the same region. Because the buyer pool is smaller and more specialized than for consumer businesses, listings often sit longer — which is an advantage if you have done the work to evaluate one properly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to buy a machine shop?

Machine shops span a wide range because the equipment itself carries real value. A small two-or-three-machine shop with the owner running the floor typically trades in the low-to-mid six figures. A shop with several CNC machining centers, multi-axis capability, an inspection room, and a management layer below the owner moves into the seven-figure range and higher. What matters more than the headline price is the split between goodwill and hard assets, because a large share of a machine shop's price is often equipment — financeable, appraisable, and recoverable in a way goodwill is not.

Is a machine shop's equipment worth more than the business?

Sometimes, and that is the most useful test a buyer can run. Commission an independent orderly-liquidation appraisal on the equipment and compare it to the asking price. If the earnings multiple implies a value below the machinery's appraised worth, you are effectively buying equipment with a business attached, which is a strong downside position. If the asking price sits far above the equipment value, you are paying for goodwill that lives in customer relationships and tribal knowledge, both of which can walk. Neither is disqualifying, but know which deal you are in before negotiating.

What is customer concentration risk in a job shop?

Job shops commonly derive a very large share of revenue from a handful of customers, sometimes the majority from one. Because most job shop work runs on purchase orders rather than long-term contracts, that revenue is not legally committed to you after closing. Ask for revenue by customer for three years, find out whether relationships sit with the departing owner or with an employee who is staying, and check whether customers maintain approved-supplier lists that require re-qualification on a change of ownership. Heavy concentration is not automatically a deal-killer, but it should reshape both price and structure — usually toward seller financing or an earnout.

Do I need to be a machinist to buy a machine shop?

No, but you need a credible answer for who runs the floor and quotes the work. Quoting is where job shops make or lose money, because a mispriced job consumes machine hours you cannot get back, and it is a skill built from experience rather than a spreadsheet. If the seller has been the estimator, that function must be replaced before closing, not after. Buyers without a machining background usually succeed by retaining a shop foreman or lead estimator on an incentive package, or by negotiating a seller transition considerably longer than the typical thirty days.

What certifications matter when buying a machine shop?

It depends on the end markets. ISO 9001 is the general quality-management baseline. AS9100 is the aerospace standard and is effectively required to quote most aerospace work. IATF 16949 covers automotive. Shops handling defense-related technical data may need ITAR registration and must control that information accordingly, and government or defense work can carry cybersecurity compliance obligations as well. Verify each certification is current, find out when the next audit falls, and confirm with the certifying body and with major customers that it survives a change of ownership rather than assuming it does.
