How to Buy a Towing Company (2026)

Towing is a fragmented, cash-flowing trade with a mix of contract-driven revenue (municipal and police rotation, highway patrol) and one-off consent tows and roadside assistance calls. It's also one of the more permit- and contract-dependent acquisitions on this list — the value of the business often lives in relationships and agreements that don't automatically transfer to a new owner. This guide covers what a towing company is worth, how contract revenue affects valuation, and what to verify before you buy.

⚡ The Short Version

What you're buying

A towing company is trucks (flatbeds, wheel-lifts, heavy-duty rotators), a storage/impound lot, driver relationships, and — usually the most valuable piece — contract relationships with municipalities, police departments, or highway patrol for rotation-based dispatch. Private-property and consent tow revenue rounds out the mix but is generally less predictable and easier for competitors to poach.

What it's worth

Small owner-operator fleets (2-5 trucks) typically price at 2x–3x SDE, in the $200,000–$800,000 range. Larger operations with municipal or rotation contracts and 10+ trucks can command 3x–4.5x EBITDA, driven by the scarcity value of contract territories.

Towing company revenue: contract vs. consent tows

Understanding the revenue mix matters more in towing than in most trades businesses, because the two main revenue streams carry very different risk profiles:

  • Contract/rotation revenue: Police, sheriff, and highway patrol rotation lists dispatch calls to a rotating panel of approved towing companies, generating predictable, marketing-free call volume. These contracts are often exclusive or semi-exclusive to a limited number of operators per zone, making them hard for new entrants to win — which is exactly what makes an existing contract valuable.
  • Consent tows and private property: Roadside assistance (often dispatched through motor clubs like AAA or insurance roadside programs), private lot enforcement, and direct consumer calls. Higher margin per call in some cases, but far more exposed to competition, review-driven reputation, and pricing pressure.

A business heavily dependent on one or two large contracts carries concentration risk — losing a single rotation slot at contract renewal can gut call volume overnight. Ask for a revenue breakdown by source for the trailing 24 months, and specifically ask when each contract is up for renewal or rebid relative to your expected close date.

What a towing company sells for

Small, owner-operator towing companies (2–5 trucks, consent and light contract work) typically sell at 2x–3x annual SDE, commonly in the $200,000–$800,000 range. Larger operations (10+ trucks, established municipal or police rotation contracts, a company-owned storage lot generating monthly fee revenue) can command 3x–4.5x EBITDA or more, priced up for the scarcity value of the contracts and the barrier to entry they represent for competitors. Factors that push valuation higher: multiple long-standing municipal or rotation contracts not up for near-term rebid, a diversified customer base across contract and consent work, a modern and well-maintained fleet (flatbeds command a premium over older wheel-lifts), and an owned rather than leased storage lot.

Factors that push valuation lower: heavy dependence on a single contract up for rebid soon, an aging fleet requiring near-term capital replacement, permits and licenses held personally by the seller rather than the business entity, and thin bookkeeping that makes SDE hard to verify (cash-heavy consent-tow revenue is a common area for under-reporting).

Permits, licenses, and contract transfer

Towing is one of the more heavily regulated trades on this list, and permit transferability should be one of your first diligence questions, not an afterthought. Requirements vary by state and municipality but commonly include a state motor carrier or towing operator license, DOT numbers and commercial registration for trucks above certain weight thresholds, a storage/impound lot license, and CDL requirements for drivers operating heavier equipment. Confirm which of these are held by the business entity versus the current owner personally — owner-held permits and licenses typically do not transfer automatically with a sale, and you may need to requalify in your own name before you can legally operate.

Municipal, police rotation, and highway patrol contracts deserve special scrutiny. Most require a separate application, background check, and approval process with the contracting agency, and some are explicitly non-transferable, meaning ownership change triggers a rebid rather than an automatic transfer. Get the specific contract language reviewed before you value the business around that revenue, and ask the seller to introduce you to the contracting agency's point of contact early in your diligence, not after you've signed a purchase agreement.

Due diligence: what to verify

Beyond standard financial diligence, towing companies carry industry-specific risks worth checking closely:

  • Contract renewal timing and transferability: Get the exact terms, renewal dates, and transfer/assignment clauses for every municipal, police, or rotation contract, in writing from the contracting agency if possible.
  • Fleet condition and age: Get an independent mechanical inspection of every truck, not just a visual walkthrough. Flatbeds and rotators are expensive to replace ($60,000-$200,000+ per unit), and deferred maintenance is a common way sellers understate near-term capital needs.
  • Storage lot compliance and lease terms: If the lot is leased, confirm the remaining term and any restrictions on impound use. If company-owned, confirm zoning compliance for vehicle storage.
  • Insurance and claims history: Towing carries meaningful liability exposure (garage keepers liability, cargo, auto liability). Review the claims history and current premium, since a poor claims record can spike insurance costs significantly for a new owner.
  • Driver retention and CDL status: Confirm which drivers hold the CDL and endorsements needed for heavier equipment, and whether they're expected to stay post-sale — losing CDL-qualified drivers right after close can idle trucks you can't legally operate yourself.
  • Cash reporting accuracy: Consent tows and private-property work are often paid in cash. Cross-check reported revenue against dispatch logs, fuel purchases, and bank deposits rather than relying solely on the seller's P&L.

Financing a towing company purchase

SBA 7(a) loans are the most common financing path, and the physical collateral value of trucks and (if owned) the storage lot generally supports lender comfort. Expect a down payment of roughly 10%–20%, with lenders paying close attention to contract concentration risk and fleet age when underwriting.

Seller financing is common, particularly for smaller owner-operator deals, and can help bridge the gap if a lender discounts contract revenue that isn't guaranteed to transfer. Equipment-specific financing through commercial truck lenders can supplement an acquisition loan if the fleet needs near-term replacement.

What makes a good towing company acquisition target

The best acquisition targets have: (1) multiple contract relationships rather than dependence on a single rotation slot, ideally with several years remaining before rebid; (2) confirmed transfer or assignment rights on those contracts, verified with the contracting agency directly; (3) a fleet that's been properly maintained, verified by independent inspection; (4) drivers with the CDL and endorsements to operate the fleet willing to stay post-sale; and (5) financials that reconcile cash-heavy consent-tow revenue against dispatch logs and bank deposits, not just a seller-prepared summary.

Red flags: a single large contract up for rebid within 12 months of your expected close, permits and licenses held personally by the seller with no clear path to your own qualification, an aging fleet with no recent capital investment, and revenue concentrated in cash consent-tow work that's difficult to verify independently.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a towing company cost to buy?

Small owner-operator towing companies (2-5 trucks) typically sell for $200,000-$800,000 at 2x-3x SDE. Larger operations with municipal or police rotation contracts and 10+ trucks can sell for $1M-$4M+ at 3x-4.5x EBITDA.

What makes a towing company valuable to buyers?

Municipal and police rotation contracts are the biggest value driver since they generate predictable call volume and are hard for competitors to win. A well-maintained fleet, owned storage lot, and diversified revenue mix also support higher multiples.

Do municipal towing contracts transfer automatically when you buy the business?

Almost never automatically. Most require a separate application and approval process with the contracting agency, and some are non-transferable and go back out to bid at ownership change.

What licenses and permits does a towing company need?

Commonly a state towing operator license, DOT/commercial registration, a storage lot license, and CDL licensing for drivers on heavier equipment. Confirm which are held by the entity versus the owner personally.
