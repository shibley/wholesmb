How to Buy a Convenience Store: The Complete Playbook

Convenience stores are one of the most commonly listed small businesses in the United States — and one of the most commonly misunderstood by first-time buyers. Headline revenue looks enormous because fuel sales inflate the top line, while the actual profit sits in a much smaller pool of inside-sales gross margin. Add cash-heavy operations, franchise and fuel-supply agreements, and potential environmental liability on fueling sites, and this becomes a category where careful diligence separates a solid cash-flowing asset from an expensive mistake. This playbook covers the real economics, what c-stores cost, how to finance one, exactly what to inspect, and the honest pros and cons.

⚡ Quick Verdict

Buy a convenience store if…

You want a daily-cash-flow retail business with a defensible location moat, you're willing to be genuinely hands-on with staffing and shrink control, and you'll underwrite the deal on inside-sales gross profit rather than total revenue.

Think twice if…

You want an absentee investment, you can't independently verify cash sales against register and supplier data, or the site has fuel and you're not prepared to pay for — and act on — a proper environmental assessment before closing.

The economics of a convenience store

A convenience store's profit and loss statement is really two businesses stacked on top of each other. Fuel, when present, generates very large revenue at a thin and volatile per-gallon margin that moves with wholesale rack prices; its main job is pulling cars onto the lot. Inside sales — packaged beverages, snacks, beer, tobacco, lottery, and prepared food — is where gross margin actually lives, with foodservice generally carrying the highest margin of the categories and tobacco among the lowest. That is why an experienced buyer looks first at inside-sales gross profit dollars per day, then at basket size and transaction count, and only then at total revenue.

Location does most of the heavy lifting. Traffic count, ease of ingress and egress, whether the store sits on the morning-commute side of the road, and how many competing stores sit within a mile all shape sales more than any operational change a new owner can make. A store with weak fundamentals in those four areas rarely gets fixed by better merchandising.

What does it cost to buy a convenience store?

Pricing splits sharply based on whether real estate and fuel are included. A small inside-only store operating in leased space commonly trades from roughly $100,000 to $400,000, typically priced around 2x–3x seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) with saleable inventory purchased separately at cost. A store with fuel operations and owned real estate usually runs $1 million to $3 million or more, but most of that figure is the land, building, canopy, and fuel infrastructure — the operating business itself may represent a modest share of the price. Branded stores under a major franchise or fuel-supply agreement carry their own transfer fees, imaging requirements, and approval process that can add real cost and time to a closing.

Financing a convenience store acquisition

Convenience store deals are well-trodden ground for acquisition lenders, but the structure depends on what's in the sale:

  • SBA 7(a) loans — the standard route for store-only or smaller mixed deals, generally requiring 10–20% down for qualified buyers with retail or management experience.
  • SBA 504 loans — frequently used when the deal includes real estate and long-lived fuel equipment, since 504 is designed for fixed-asset acquisition and often carries a longer amortization.
  • Seller financing — common on independent, owner-operated stores, and particularly valuable here because a seller willing to carry paper is implicitly standing behind the numbers they reported.
  • Fuel supplier incentives — on branded fuel sites, distributors sometimes contribute to imaging or equipment costs in exchange for a multi-year supply commitment; read those terms carefully before treating them as free money.

What to inspect before you buy

C-store diligence is unusually document-heavy because so much of the revenue is cash and so much of the risk sits underground or in contracts.

  • Point-of-sale and register data — pull at least 24 months of daily sales reports by department and reconcile them against bank deposits and tax returns. A seller who can only produce a summary spreadsheet is telling you something.
  • Supplier purchase records — invoices from beverage, snack, and tobacco distributors provide an independent cross-check on claimed sales volume that is much harder to inflate than a P&L.
  • Environmental condition — on any fuel site, commission a Phase I environmental site assessment, and a Phase II if it flags anything. Underground storage tank contamination can create remediation costs that exceed the purchase price, and liability often follows ownership.
  • Fuel supply and franchise agreements — review term length, minimum volume commitments, imaging obligations, transfer fees, and whether the brand must approve you as a buyer.
  • Licenses — confirm exactly how beer/wine, tobacco, and lottery licenses transfer in that state and county, how long approval takes, and whether any are non-transferable and must be reapplied for.
  • Lease terms — if real estate is not included, remaining term and renewal options are the whole ballgame; a short lease with no options makes the business nearly unfinanceable and unsellable.
  • Shrink and staffing — ask for inventory variance history, understand current wage rates and turnover, and account for the reality that most c-stores run on hourly staff in a high-turnover labor market.

Pros and cons

👍 Pros

  • Daily cash flow with fast inventory turns and no receivables to chase.
  • Location creates a genuine local moat that competitors can't easily replicate.
  • Steady, largely non-discretionary demand across economic cycles.
  • Well-understood by SBA lenders, with real estate options that support long-amortization financing.

👎 Cons

  • Cash-heavy operations make revenue verification harder than in most categories.
  • Environmental liability on fuel sites can be severe and is not always visible up front.
  • Long hours, high staff turnover, and hands-on management — rarely a passive asset.
  • Thin fuel margins and declining tobacco volumes pressure two traditional revenue pillars.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to buy a convenience store?

A small inside-only store leasing its space commonly trades for roughly $100,000 to $400,000, often priced around 2x–3x seller's discretionary earnings plus inventory at cost. A store with fuel and owned real estate usually runs $1 million to $3 million or more, because most of that price is the land, building and fuel infrastructure rather than the operating business.

Do convenience stores make more money on fuel or inside sales?

Inside sales. Fuel typically drives volume and foot traffic at a thin per-gallon margin, while packaged beverages, snacks, tobacco, and especially foodservice carry much higher gross margins. Underwrite a fuel-and-store deal on inside-sales gross profit, not on total revenue, which is inflated by fuel dollars.

Can you get an SBA loan to buy a convenience store?

Yes. SBA 7(a) is common for store-only acquisitions and SBA 504 is often used when real estate and fuel equipment are included. Lenders will scrutinize environmental condition of underground storage tanks on fuel sites, and typically want a buyer with retail or management experience.

What's the biggest risk in buying a convenience store?

Unverifiable cash sales and environmental liability. Many c-stores run significant cash volume, so revenue claims must be tied back to register data, supplier purchase records and tax returns. On fuel sites, contamination from underground storage tanks can create cleanup costs that dwarf the purchase price, so a Phase I environmental assessment is not optional.
