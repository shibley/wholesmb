How to Buy a Dry Cleaning Business (2026)

Dry cleaning is a classic "boring business" with recurring local demand and an aging owner base looking to retire, but it comes with an industry-specific wrinkle most buyers underestimate: environmental liability tied to the cleaning solvent itself. This guide covers what a dry cleaning business is worth, the difference between a plant and a drop store, how to find one for sale, and what to verify — especially environmentally — before you sign.

⚡ The Short Version

What you're buying

Either a full-service plant (on-site cleaning equipment, environmental permitting, higher margin) or a drop store (retail front only, garments shipped to a third-party plant, lower margin but no equipment or environmental risk). Understanding which one you're evaluating changes the entire due diligence process.

What it's worth

Drop stores typically price at 1.5x–2.5x SDE. Plants price at 2x–3x SDE, with environmental history — not just financial performance — a major swing factor in what a buyer should actually be willing to pay.

Plant vs. drop store: two different businesses

The single most important distinction before evaluating any dry cleaning listing is whether it's a plant or a drop store, because the economics and risk profile are completely different:

  • Full-service plant: Owns the cleaning machinery on-site and processes garments directly, often for its own storefront plus wholesale contracts from other drop stores in the area. Higher margin since there's no third-party cleaning fee, but requires environmental permitting, solvent handling compliance, and equipment maintenance/replacement capital.
  • Drop store: A retail-only location that collects garments and ships them to a plant (sometimes owned by the same operator, often not) for cleaning. Lower capital requirements and no direct environmental liability, but margin is compressed by the wholesale cleaning fee paid to the plant.

Some sellers own both a plant and a network of drop-store locations feeding it — that combination commands a premium since it captures margin at both stages and diversifies revenue across multiple storefronts.

What a dry cleaning business sells for

Single-location drop stores typically sell at 1.5x–2.5x SDE, often in the $50,000–$200,000 range, reflecting lower capital intensity but also lower margin and higher sensitivity to losing the wholesale cleaning relationship. Full-service plants, especially those with multiple drop-store routes feeding them, typically sell at 2x–3x SDE, often $200,000–$800,000, with well-documented environmental compliance and modern, non-perc equipment supporting the higher end of that range. Factors that push valuation higher: a diversified commercial account base (hotels, uniforms, restaurants) alongside retail customers, modern or alternative-solvent equipment, and a documented clean environmental history. Factors that push it lower: aging perc equipment nearing replacement, thin commercial contracts, and any history of environmental violations or unresolved contamination.

Where to find dry cleaning businesses for sale

BizBuySell has the largest broker-listed inventory of dry cleaning businesses nationwide, often filterable by plant vs. drop-store format and by whether equipment uses perc or an alternative solvent. Regional business brokers who specialize in the garment care and laundry trade are also a common source, since many retiring owners sell through relationships built over years rather than a public listing.

Equipment distributors and industry trade associations (such as regional drycleaning and laundry institutes) are useful off-market sources and can also help you sanity-check the age and replacement cost of a target's equipment before you make an offer.

Due diligence: what to verify

Dry cleaning has a genuinely unusual risk profile compared to most small-business acquisitions. Protect yourself with these verification steps:

  • Environmental site assessment (plants only): Commission a Phase I environmental site assessment before making an offer on any plant, and a Phase II (soil/groundwater testing) if the Phase I flags any concern. Perc contamination liability can attach to the property and, in some structures, follow the business even after a change of ownership — this is the single biggest risk in the category and is not optional diligence.
  • Solvent type and equipment age: Confirm whether the plant uses perchloroethylene (perc) or an alternative solvent (hydrocarbon, GreenEarth/siloxane, wet cleaning). Perc equipment faces tightening state and local regulation, and some jurisdictions are phasing it out entirely — factor likely replacement costs into your offer if equipment is older or perc-based.
  • Underground and aboveground storage tanks: Identify any current or historical solvent storage tanks on the property and confirm proper registration, inspection history, and closure documentation for any decommissioned tanks.
  • Commercial account concentration: Request a breakdown of wholesale/commercial revenue (hotels, restaurants, uniform services) versus retail walk-in revenue, and confirm whether commercial contracts are formalized or handshake relationships that could walk with the seller.
  • Drop-store network stability: For a plant with feeder drop stores, confirm which stores are owned outright, which are independently owned wholesale customers, and how likely each is to stay post-sale — losing a drop-store relationship directly cuts plant volume.
  • Equipment condition and remaining useful life: Get an independent inspection of pressing equipment, boilers, and cleaning machines. Deferred maintenance and near-end-of-life equipment are common ways sellers understate near-term capital needs.
  • Local and state permits: Confirm air quality, wastewater discharge, and hazardous materials handling permits are current and transferable, since dry cleaning is one of the more heavily regulated small-business categories at the state and local level.

Financing a dry cleaning business purchase

SBA 7(a) loans are available for dry cleaning acquisitions, but lenders scrutinize environmental risk closely for plants — expect to need a clean Phase I assessment (and Phase II if flagged) as a condition of financing, and budget for that cost during due diligence rather than assuming it's optional. Drop stores, carrying no direct environmental exposure, are generally easier to finance conventionally. Expect to put down roughly 10%–20%, with plants toward the higher end given the added underwriting complexity.

Seller financing is common in this category, particularly for smaller owner-operator deals, and can help bridge any gap between what a bank will lend against and the asking price once environmental diligence costs are factored in.

What makes a good dry cleaning acquisition target

Not every dry cleaning business is worth buying at any price. The best acquisition targets have: (1) a clean, documented environmental history with no unresolved contamination issues, verified independently rather than taken on the seller's word; (2) modern or alternative-solvent equipment, or a clear, budgeted plan for replacement; (3) a diversified customer base spanning both retail and commercial accounts; (4) for plants, a stable network of feeder drop stores; and (5) clean, verifiable financials with commercial and retail revenue tracked separately.

Red flags: any history of environmental violations, spills, or unresolved regulatory action; aging perc equipment with no replacement plan in an area tightening solvent regulation; heavy dependency on one or two commercial accounts; and a seller unwilling to provide or pay for a Phase I environmental assessment before closing, which should be treated as a serious warning sign rather than a negotiable line item.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a dry cleaning business cost to buy?

Single-location drop stores commonly sell for $50,000–$200,000, priced at 1.5x–2.5x SDE. Full-service plants with on-site cleaning equipment and multiple drop-store routes typically sell for $200,000–$800,000, priced at 2x–3x SDE, with environmental liability history a major swing factor in final price.

What's the difference between a dry cleaning plant and a drop store?

A plant does the actual cleaning on-site with its own machinery and holds the associated environmental permitting and liability. A drop store is a retail front that collects garments and ships them to a third-party plant for cleaning, avoiding equipment costs and environmental risk but paying wholesale cleaning fees that compress margin.

What environmental risks come with buying a dry cleaning plant?

Traditional dry cleaning uses perchloroethylene (perc), a solvent classified as a likely human carcinogen with strict handling, storage, and disposal rules. Historical spills or improper disposal can create contamination liability that transfers with the property. A Phase I (and if warranted Phase II) environmental site assessment is essential before buying any plant.

Where can I find dry cleaning businesses for sale?

BizBuySell carries the largest inventory of dry cleaning businesses listed by brokers nationwide, often filterable by plant vs. drop-store format. Industry associations and equipment distributors are also common sources of off-market deals.
