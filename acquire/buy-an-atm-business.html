How to Buy an ATM Business (2026): Route Economics and Diligence

An ATM route is often pitched as passive income, and it is genuinely one of the less labor-intensive small businesses you can own. But the pitch skips two things that decide whether a route is a good buy: the capital that sits inside the machines rather than in your account, and the fact that a route’s value lives almost entirely in its location agreements rather than in its hardware. This guide covers how the revenue is actually earned, what routes trade for, how much cash the business consumes, the compliance obligations that come with operating terminals, and the terminal-level diligence that verifies the numbers before you sign.

⚡ The Short Version

What you're buying

A set of terminals placed under agreements with location owners, the processing relationship those terminals settle through, and the transaction history that supports the price. The machines themselves have modest resale value; the agreements and the volume they generate are the asset. A route where the seller’s personal relationship is the agreement is worth materially less than one with assignable written contracts.

What it's worth

A modest multiple of verified annual net profit — net of the location split, processing, cash logistics, maintenance, and communications. Price the route off terminal-level settlement reports, not a seller summary, and add vault cash to your capital plan separately. It is a working-capital business dressed as an equipment business.

How ATM route economics actually work

Revenue arrives from two places. The surcharge is the convenience fee the terminal charges the cardholder at the point of withdrawal, disclosed on screen and on the machine, and set by the operator within network rules. Interchange is a smaller per-transaction payment that flows to the ATM operator from the cardholder’s issuing bank through the network. Both are earned per transaction, which means the entire business is a volume business: the number of withdrawals a location generates matters far more than anything you can do to the machine.

From gross revenue per transaction, several costs come off before anything reaches you:

  • The location split — most site owners take a share of the surcharge, and the percentage varies enormously with the site’s bargaining power. A busy bar or convenience store knows what its foot traffic is worth; a small office building usually does not.
  • Processing and network fees — charged per transaction and sometimes per terminal per month by the processor or independent sales organization that sponsors your terminals onto the networks.
  • Cash logistics — either your own time and vehicle cost to load machines, or an armored carrier contract if the route is large enough or the locations risky enough to justify one.
  • Communications — each terminal needs connectivity, commonly wireless, at a recurring monthly cost per machine.
  • Maintenance and compliance upgrades — dispenser and card-reader servicing, plus periodic hardware or software requirements imposed by the card networks, which are not optional and arrive on someone else’s schedule.
  • The cost of the cash — whether it is your capital sitting idle or a vault-cash arrangement with a lender, the money in the machine has a cost.

What remains is a small margin multiplied by transaction count. That structure has an important consequence for diligence: a route’s profit is almost always concentrated. Pull the per-terminal data and you will typically find that a minority of machines produce the majority of the net, and a tail of machines barely cover their communications and service cost. You are really buying the top of that distribution, and you should price the tail at close to nothing.

What ATM routes sell for

Routes are priced as a multiple of annual net profit, and the multiple sits at the lower end of the small-business range for a reason: the hardware has limited liquidation value, the customer relationships are terminable, and the business does not scale without proportional capital. A route with written, assignable, multi-year location agreements, connected terminals producing clean settlement reporting, an existing armored-carrier arrangement, and no single site above a modest share of profit earns the top of the range. A route of verbal handshakes with machines the seller personally services earns the bottom, because on the day the seller leaves, several of those handshakes leave with them.

The most common pricing error buyers make is anchoring on gross surcharge revenue. Gross revenue on an ATM route can look impressive relative to the asking price while net profit after the location split, processing, connectivity, and cash logistics is a fraction of it. Insist on being shown net, and rebuild it yourself from the settlement reports and the actual expense invoices rather than accepting the seller’s cost assumptions. Our valuation guide covers the general method; here the discipline is simply refusing to pay a going-concern multiple on revenue that has not yet had its real costs deducted.

Vault cash: the capital nobody mentions in the listing

Every machine on the route needs to be stocked, and that money is your working capital. It sits in a cassette earning nothing, it is exposed to theft and to loss in transit, and it is fully committed for as long as you own the route. The total requirement scales with the number of terminals, the amount each dispenses between service visits, and how often you can realistically refill them — a tighter service cadence means less cash tied up but more of your time and fuel spent.

There are vault-cash arrangements in which a lender or a specialist provider supplies the currency and charges for it, which converts a capital requirement into an operating cost. Whether that is worth it depends on your alternative use of the money and on the terms you can get, but you should model both cases explicitly. The point for a buyer is simply this: the asking price is not the capital requirement. Adding a realistic vault-cash figure to the purchase price frequently changes the return profile of a route from attractive to ordinary, and that calculation belongs in your model before you make an offer, not after.

Location agreements and merchant relationships

The location agreement is the asset. Read every one, and specifically confirm four things: that it is in writing, that it is assignable to you without the site owner’s unilateral right to renegotiate, what the split is and whether it escalates, and what the term and exclusivity provisions say. An exclusivity clause matters more than buyers expect, because a competing operator placing a second machine in the same venue halves the value of yours overnight.

Then look past the paper at the relationship. Ask to visit every meaningful location with the seller and meet whoever actually decides. On a route of any size, a handful of site contacts carry most of the profit, and their willingness to keep the machine after the seller leaves is the single largest unpriced risk in the deal. Structuring part of the price as a seller-financed note or an earnout tied to trailing transaction volume aligns that risk properly — see our guide on seller financing for how those terms are usually written.

Compliance, registration and operational requirements

Operating ATMs is a regulated activity, and the obligations vary by state and by the sponsor you settle through. Independent ATM operators are commonly subject to registration requirements, know-your-customer and anti-money-laundering obligations imposed through their sponsor or processor, and network operating rules covering fee disclosure, accessibility, and terminal hardware and software standards. Some states impose their own licensing or registration on operators; others do not. Because this is jurisdiction-specific and changes, confirm the current requirements with your processor and a local attorney rather than relying on what the seller has historically done.

Two practical diligence items follow. First, confirm the terminals on the route currently meet network hardware, software, and accessibility requirements, and ask the processor whether any upcoming mandate will require an upgrade — a fleet-wide compliance upgrade shortly after closing is a real and avoidable surprise. Second, confirm the seller’s registrations, sponsorship, and filings are in order and understand which of them transfer and which you must obtain yourself. Neither is expensive to check; both are expensive to discover late. The general legal, tax, and contract review in our due diligence checklist applies here as it does to any acquisition.

How to verify the revenue

The verification path on an ATM route is unusually clean, which means there is no excuse for skipping it. Every transaction settles through a processor, and that processor produces terminal-level reporting. Request twelve to twenty-four months of it per terminal, then:

  • Reconcile settlements to the bank. Processor reports should tie to deposits in the seller’s account. If they do not, find out why before anything else.
  • Rebuild net per terminal. Apply each location’s actual split, the processing fees from the invoices, and the connectivity cost per machine. Rank the terminals by net. Price the route on that ranking.
  • Look at the trend, not the total. Twenty-four months shows whether transaction counts are flat, growing, or eroding. In locations where card and mobile payment acceptance has increased, cash withdrawal volume can decline steadily, and a route’s trailing twelve months may already overstate next year.
  • Check seasonality and events. Routes weighted toward venues, festivals, or seasonal trade have a peak that is not an annual run rate.
  • Ride the route. Service the machines with the seller once. You will learn the drive time, the actual condition of the terminals, the traffic at each site, and whether the locations are where the schedule says they are.

Financing an ATM route purchase

Smaller routes are usually cash or seller-financed transactions, and seller financing is both common and appropriate here given how much of the value rests on relationships the seller controls. For larger routes, SBA 7(a) acquisition lending is available for established, profitable businesses with clean books, though lenders scrutinize goodwill-heavy deals with low-collateral-value equipment more carefully than they do asset-backed purchases. Be prepared to demonstrate the terminal-level settlement history rather than a summary, since that is the evidence a lender will underwrite.

Note that vault cash generally will not be funded as part of an acquisition loan in the way equipment or goodwill is, so plan for it out of your own capital or through a dedicated vault-cash arrangement. What makes a good ATM route acquisition

The routes worth buying share a shape: written and assignable location agreements with real remaining term; no single site carrying an outsized share of net profit; locations whose customers still need cash for a structural reason rather than out of habit, such as venues with cash-preferred vendors, laundromats, or businesses that surcharge card payments; terminals that already meet current network requirements; a tight geographic cluster so service runs are short; and a seller willing to introduce you to every site contact and to carry a note.

The red flags mirror those exactly. Handshake placements. One venue producing most of the profit. Terminals facing a known compliance upgrade. Locations that have recently added card acceptance or moved toward cashless operation. A route spread thin enough that servicing it costs more in time than the marginal machines earn. And a seller who offers a revenue summary they compiled themselves and resists giving you processor-level reporting — in a business where the settlement data exists and is easy to produce, reluctance to share it is the finding.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does an ATM business make money?

Two streams: the surcharge paid by the cardholder at the machine, and interchange paid to the operator by the cardholder’s bank through the network. From the combined per-transaction figure you deduct the location owner’s share, processing and network fees, cash-loading or armored carrier cost, connectivity, maintenance, and the cost of the cash itself. The margin per transaction is small, so the business is entirely about transaction volume.

How much does it cost to buy an ATM route?

Routes trade at a modest multiple of verified annual net profit, not of gross surcharge revenue. Small routes are often sold directly by operators without a broker; larger routes with written contracts, connected terminals, and established cash logistics command higher multiples. Whatever the price, add vault cash to your capital plan separately — the purchase price is not the total requirement.

How much cash do you need to stock the machines?

Vault cash scales with the number of terminals, how much each dispenses between fills, and how often you service them. On a route of any size it can rival or exceed the purchase price. It earns nothing while it sits in a cassette and is exposed to loss, so a more frequent service cadence trades your time for less committed capital. Vault-cash arrangements exist that convert the requirement into an operating cost; model both.

How do I verify a route’s transaction volume?

Get terminal-level settlement reporting from the processor covering at least twelve months, and reconcile it to the bank account the settlements landed in. Then rebuild net profit per terminal using each location’s actual split and the real expense invoices. A seller who can only supply a self-compiled summary is asking you to buy an assertion in a business where the underlying data demonstrably exists.

What are the biggest risks in buying an ATM route?

Verbal or non-assignable location agreements that do not survive the sale, concentration in one or two sites, terminals facing a network-mandated hardware or software upgrade, the working capital and loss exposure of vault cash, declining cash usage in the venues served, and operator registration and anti-money-laundering obligations that vary by state and sponsor. All are discoverable before closing.
