Businesses for Sale in Montana: Buyer's Guide 2026

Montana pairs no state sales tax with an outdoor-recreation economy that has drawn sustained in-migration and rising incomes to Bozeman and Missoula, while Big Sky, Yellowstone, and Glacier National Park anchor a tourism corridor that spans the western half of the state. Billings remains the state's largest commercial hub and energy/agriculture trade center, and the rest of Montana's economy leans on ranching, agriculture, and the trades that support it. This guide covers where to search, what industries dominate around Bozeman, Missoula, and Big Sky, what deals cost, and the state-specific quirks that shape a Montana acquisition.

What to expect on price

Established Bozeman and Missoula businesses often trade at 3x–4x SDE given the metros' growth and buyer demand, above the national average, while Billings and smaller regional-hub businesses typically land closer to 2.5x–3x SDE. Seasonal Big Sky and Yellowstone-adjacent tourism businesses often trade at a discount unless the seller can document consistent shoulder-season cash flow.

Why buyers target Montana

Montana's pitch to business buyers starts with no state sales tax and a quality-of-life story that has pulled in remote workers, retirees, and relocating professionals for the better part of a decade — Bozeman in particular has seen some of the fastest population and income growth of any small metro in the country. For a buyer, that growth translates into rising demand for local services: contractors, healthcare, professional services, and hospitality all benefit from a customer base that keeps expanding faster than the state's overall population would suggest.

Regionally, Bozeman and Missoula anchor the state's growth story, both driven by outdoor recreation, university-adjacent economies (Montana State and University of Montana), and an influx of higher-income remote workers. Big Sky, West Yellowstone, and the Glacier National Park gateway towns run on a tourism economy with sharp winter-ski and summer-park seasonality. Billings remains the state's largest metro and its commercial, energy, and agricultural trade hub, while the rest of the state is largely ranching and agriculture, supporting a steady base of trades and equipment businesses. Buyers should model Montana's small overall population and buyer pool outside these hubs, rising real estate costs in Bozeman and Missoula specifically, and real seasonality for any tourism-dependent business.

Most common business types for sale in Montana

Montana's business-for-sale market splits between Bozeman/Missoula growth, Big Sky/Yellowstone tourism, and Billings-anchored agriculture and energy:

  • Outdoor-recreation and hospitality businesses — guiding services, lodging, retail, and restaurants near Big Sky, Yellowstone, Glacier, and the broader ski and fly-fishing tourism economy.
  • Construction and real-estate-services firms — contractors, property management, and trades businesses riding Bozeman and Missoula's sustained population growth.
  • Agricultural and ranching-adjacent companies — equipment dealers, livestock services, and farm-supply businesses tied to the state's cattle and grain economy.
  • Trades businesses — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and general contracting, in high demand statewide as both population grows and owners retire.
  • Healthcare-adjacent practices — dental, veterinary, and home care agencies serving both growing metros and rural populations.
  • Energy and industrial-services businesses — oilfield services, trucking, and equipment rental concentrated around Billings and eastern Montana.

Valuation: what Montana businesses sell for

Pricing follows the same general framework as the rest of the U.S. — a multiple of seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) — but Montana shows a wider spread than most states. Established Bozeman and Missoula businesses commonly command 3x–4x SDE, above the national average, driven by strong local demand and a limited supply of quality listings in those metros. Billings and smaller regional-hub businesses tend to trade closer to 2.5x–3x SDE, more in line with national norms. Seasonal Big Sky and Yellowstone-adjacent tourism businesses are the exception: buyers and lenders discount revenue concentrated in winter-ski or summer-park seasons, so multiples for those businesses often land lower unless the seller can document several consecutive years of consistent shoulder-season performance.

Always normalize the financials yourself, and for any tourism-dependent business, request month-by-month revenue for at least the trailing two to three years — a single strong or weak ski or park season can distort a single annual figure.

Financing a Montana business purchase

SBA 7(a) loans remain the most common financing path for established, profitable businesses. Montana's community banks and credit unions, particularly in Bozeman, Missoula, and Billings, are active in acquisition lending, though the fastest-growing metros have also seen rising real estate costs push up total deal size for businesses with a real property component. Seasonal Big Sky and Yellowstone-adjacent tourism businesses can be harder to finance through conventional channels since lenders want to see the business can service debt through the off-season — sellers of these businesses are more likely to offer seller financing to bridge that gap. You'll typically need roughly 10% down, solid personal credit, and at least two years of verifiable earnings for a standard SBA deal.

Montana-specific due diligence checklist

Standard due diligence applies everywhere, but Montana has a few state-specific wrinkles worth flagging early:

  • Ski and park-season concentration — for any Big Sky, Yellowstone, or Glacier-adjacent hospitality business, verify how much annual revenue depends on the winter-ski and summer-park peak weeks specifically, not just the broader season.
  • Water rights — Montana water law is notoriously complex; for any business with agricultural or rural real estate, confirm water rights are clearly documented, senior in priority, and transferable.
  • Rising real estate costs — in Bozeman and Missoula specifically, confirm whether the sale price reflects appreciated real estate value versus the underlying business's earning power, and get an independent appraisal if real property is included.
  • Agricultural and ranching cyclicality — for businesses serving ranchers and farmers, factor in commodity-price cycles and weather-driven swings in the local customer base's spending power.
  • Workforce and housing availability — Bozeman and Missoula's housing costs have made staffing harder for service businesses; confirm the seller's key employees intend to stay and that housing isn't a hidden retention risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many businesses are for sale in Montana?

BizBuySell carries a steady but modest inventory of Montana businesses, concentrated around Bozeman, Missoula, and the Big Sky/Yellowstone tourism corridor, with a smaller pool in Billings and Kalispell.

What types of businesses sell most often in Montana?

Outdoor-recreation and hospitality businesses near Big Sky and Yellowstone, construction and real-estate-services firms riding Bozeman and Missoula's growth, agricultural and ranching-adjacent trades, and Main Street retail in regional hub towns are the most commonly listed categories.

Is Montana a good state to buy a small business?

Montana has no state sales tax and strong in-migration to Bozeman and Missoula, which supports demand for local services. Trade-offs include a small overall buyer pool, tourism seasonality, and rising real estate costs in the fastest-growing metros.

Can I use an SBA loan to buy a Montana business?

Yes. SBA 7(a) loans are available statewide, and Montana's community banks and credit unions, particularly in Bozeman, Missoula, and Billings, are active in acquisition lending.
