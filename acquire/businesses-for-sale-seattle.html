Businesses for Sale in Seattle: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Seattle is a high-income metro with an expensive labor market and a tax structure that behaves differently from almost anywhere else in the country. Washington has no personal or corporate income tax, but it levies a business and occupation tax on gross receipts — which means a low-margin business here can owe tax in a year it makes no profit. Layer on one of the highest minimum wages in the United States and commercial rents to match, and the gap between a seller's historical cost structure and yours can be wider in Seattle than in most markets. This guide covers where Puget Sound listings live, which categories move, and the diligence that actually matters in Washington.

Where businesses for sale in Seattle are listed

BizBuySell is the primary aggregator for Puget Sound, and you should search the region rather than the city — Bellevue, Redmond, Kirkland, Renton, Kent, Lynnwood, Everett, and Tacoma share one labor market, and many service businesses that list as "Seattle" actually run crews from a yard in the south end or on the Eastside. Washington supports an established independent business-broker community, and in the trades a meaningful share of the better inventory is worked quietly through brokers before it is widely advertised, so relationships with two or three brokers in your target category pay off. For businesses that don't need a physical Washington presence, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere.

Popular industries for sale in Seattle

The Puget Sound listing mix reflects an affluent metro with an older housing stock, a long wet season, and a dense professional workforce:

  • Home & property services — plumbing, electrical, roofing, and landscaping, all supported by pre-war and mid-century housing that needs continuous work.
  • Exterior maintenance — pressure washing, gutter service, and moss and roof treatment, a genuinely recurring category in a climate this wet.
  • Food & beverage — coffee shops, restaurants, and bars, concentrated in Capitol Hill, Ballard, Fremont, and along the Eastside retail corridors.
  • Fitness, wellness & pets — gyms and studios, med spas, and pet grooming, all strong in a high-income, high pet-ownership metro.
  • Auto & storage — auto repair, car washes, and self-storage, supported by a suburban footprint and small-square-footage urban housing.
  • B2B & professional services — accounting firms, insurance agencies, commercial cleaning, and property management companies serving a large rental base.

Puget Sound submarkets to watch

The Eastside — Bellevue, Redmond, Kirkland, Sammamish, Issaquah — holds the highest household incomes in the region and prices consumer and home-service businesses accordingly, with strong maintenance rather than repair demand. Seattle proper carries the food, bar, and boutique-retail inventory at the highest rents, the fastest turnover, and the strictest city-level employment rules. South King County, running through Renton, Kent, Auburn, and Federal Way, is where the light-industrial, trade, and owner-operated Main Street inventory sits, and it generally offers the best earnings-to-price ratios in the metro. North through Lynnwood and Everett trends more affordable and more service-oriented, with a manufacturing-linked customer base. Tacoma and Pierce County are cheaper again and function as a partly separate market — worth searching, but don't assume a Seattle-area crew can serve it profitably given regional traffic.

How to evaluate a Seattle business listing

Start with the B&O tax. Washington's business and occupation tax applies to gross receipts, not net income, and the rate varies by activity classification — retailing, wholesaling, and service are taxed differently. There is no deduction for cost of goods sold, so a high-revenue, thin-margin business carries a tax burden that has no analogue in an income-tax state. If you are comparing a Seattle listing to one in Oregon or Idaho, you cannot read the two P&Ls the same way. Confirm which classification the business files under, whether it has been filing correctly, and whether any city-level B&O applies on top, because Seattle, Bellevue, Tacoma, and several other municipalities levy their own.

Second, model labor at your cost, not the seller's. Seattle's minimum wage is among the highest in the country and rises annually, and the city's secure-scheduling ordinance imposes advance-notice and predictability-pay requirements on larger food-service and retail employers. A business that was grandfathered into a favorable staffing pattern, or that has long-tenured staff paid below what a replacement would cost, can look considerably more profitable on paper than it will under new ownership. Ask for a current roster with tenure and wage, then reprice it as if you had to rehire the whole team.

Third, check client concentration against the tech sector. Commercial cleaning, catering, corporate services, and even some trade businesses in this metro carry heavy exposure to a handful of large employers, and hybrid-work shifts have already reset demand for anything tied to office occupancy. Ask for revenue by client for the last three years and look at the trend, not just the total. Then confirm state and city licensing transfers, verify contractor registration and bonding with Washington L&I, and read commercial leases closely. Financing a Seattle acquisition

SBA 7(a) loans remain the standard acquisition route and typically cover 70–90% of the purchase price where financials are clean and documented. Washington has a deep bench of SBA-preferred lenders, and Puget Sound service businesses with recurring maintenance or commercial contracts underwrite well. Expect underwriters to look hard at labor cost assumptions in this market — a projection that holds payroll flat through a minimum-wage increase will get repriced during underwriting, and the loan will be sized off the lender's number rather than yours. Where the deal includes real estate, an SBA 504 alongside the 7(a) is usually the better structure, though Seattle-area commercial property prices put owner-occupied purchases out of reach for many Main Street buyers. Seller financing is common and worth pushing for, particularly on businesses with concentrated client revenue where a seller carrying paper signals confidence those accounts will stay.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Seattle?

BizBuySell has the deepest Puget Sound coverage — search the region rather than the city, since Bellevue, Redmond, Kirkland, Renton, Kent, and Everett share one market. Washington has an established independent broker community, and in the trades much of the better inventory moves through brokers before it's widely advertised. For remote-run businesses, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are most commonly for sale in Seattle?

Home and property services lead — plumbing, electrical, roofing, landscaping, pressure washing and moss treatment — followed by coffee shops, restaurants and bars, gyms and studios, pet services, auto repair, self-storage, and professional services.

How much do businesses for sale in Seattle typically cost?

Main Street businesses commonly list from roughly $150,000 to $1 million, with established multi-crew trade companies frequently exceeding that. Pricing generally runs 2.5x–4x SDE, with the higher multiples reserved for recurring service contracts and B2B revenue rather than walk-in retail or food service.

What should I watch out for when buying a business in Seattle?

Check the B&O tax treatment first — Washington taxes gross receipts rather than profit, and several cities levy their own on top, which changes thin-margin economics. Then reprice the payroll at current wage levels rather than the seller's historical cost, check client concentration against tech-sector employers, and verify that L&I contractor registration and city licensing transfer.
