How to Buy a Moving Company (2026)

Moving companies combine recurring commercial contracts with seasonal residential demand, but the category is also asset-heavy, regulation-heavy, and dependent on a stable driver workforce. This guide covers what a moving company is worth, what drives that valuation, franchise vs. independent economics, and what to verify before you sign.

⚡ The Short Version

What you're buying

A moving company is a fleet-and-labor business built on trucks, equipment, DOT/MC operating authority, and a roster of drivers and movers. You're buying the vehicle fleet and its remaining useful life, the customer base (residential, commercial, or both), any interstate operating authority, and the safety/insurance record that determines your ongoing liability costs. Driver retention and CDL availability matter more than most buyers expect in a tight labor market.

What it's worth

Small local operators typically price at 2x–3x SDE. Established regional companies with interstate authority, a diversified commercial account base, and a well-maintained fleet can command a premium within that range or above it, particularly when real estate (a truck yard or warehouse) is included.

Moving company economics: residential vs. commercial revenue

Understanding the revenue mix is the single most important step before evaluating any specific moving company. Revenue splits into distinct categories with very different risk profiles:

  • Residential local moves: The bread-and-butter of most small movers, heavily seasonal (peak May–September) and driven by local referrals, review sites, and lead-gen platforms.
  • Residential long-distance/interstate moves: Higher ticket size but requires active DOT/MC interstate operating authority and more complex compliance (weight tickets, tariff filings).
  • Commercial & office relocations: The most valuable revenue stream to a buyer — less seasonal, higher ticket, and often repeat business tied to corporate relocation contracts or property-management relationships.
  • Specialty moves & storage: Piano moving, fine art, and add-on storage services carry higher margins and differentiate an operator from low-cost competitors.

A moving company that's 90%+ dependent on residential local moves booked through third-party lead platforms (which take a meaningful cut per lead) is often less defensible than one with direct commercial relationships and repeat corporate accounts. Ask for a 24-month revenue breakdown by category, plus the lead source mix (direct/referral vs. paid lead platforms).

What a moving company sells for

Small local operators (2–5 trucks, single metro) typically sell at 2x–3x annual SDE, often in the $150,000–$600,000 range. Established regional companies with interstate authority, a larger fleet, diversified commercial accounts, and multiple years of clean financials can sell for $500,000–$2M or more, with fleet size, real estate (truck yard/warehouse), and commercial account concentration driving the higher end. Factors that push valuation higher: a well-maintained fleet with low deferred maintenance, active DOT/MC authority in good standing with a clean safety rating, repeat commercial contracts, and a stable driver roster that isn't dependent on one owner-operator recruiting all the labor.

Factors that push valuation lower: an aging fleet needing near-term capital replacement, a poor DOT safety rating or open compliance issues, heavy dependence on third-party lead platforms, high driver turnover, and revenue concentrated in a short peak season with thin off-season cash flow.

Franchise vs. independent: which is the better buy?

Franchise movers (Two Men and a Truck, College Hunks Hauling Junk & Moving, and similar) come with brand recognition, centralized lead generation, proven operating systems, and national account relationships. The trade-off is ongoing royalty and marketing fees and a franchisor approval process for the transfer, which can add weeks to closing. Independent moving companies have no royalty drag and more pricing and service flexibility, but growth depends more heavily on the current owner's local reputation, referral network, and direct commercial relationships — a risk if that owner isn't staying involved during transition.

DOT/MC operating authority: what transfers and what doesn't

If the moving company operates across state lines, it holds Motor Carrier (MC) operating authority and a USDOT number issued by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration. Operating authority is generally tied to the business entity, not the individual owner, so an asset purchase (buying trucks and customer lists without the entity) typically requires you to apply for new authority, which can take weeks and reset the carrier's safety rating history. An entity purchase (buying the company itself) can preserve existing authority and safety history, which is often more valuable than the physical assets alone. Confirm which structure the deal uses early, since it materially affects your timeline to operate legally after closing.

Where to find moving companies for sale

BizBuySell lists both independent moving companies and franchise resales nationwide and is the best starting point for a broad search. If you're specifically interested in a franchise brand, most major moving franchisors (Two Men and a Truck, College Hunks) maintain their own resale process for existing territories — check directly with the franchisor's development team. Industry associations such as the American Moving & Storage Association, commercial truck and equipment dealers, and moving-industry business brokers are also common sources of off-market leads.

Due diligence: what to verify

Moving companies have unique risk factors beyond standard financial due diligence. Protect yourself with these verification steps:

  • DOT safety rating and compliance history: Pull the carrier's FMCSA safety record (SAFER system) and review any out-of-service orders, violations, or pending compliance actions. A poor safety rating can affect insurability and interstate operating rights after you take over.
  • Fleet condition and replacement cost: Get an itemized fleet list with age, mileage, and an independent mechanical inspection. Moving trucks have a real replacement cost (often $60,000–$150,000+ per truck), and deferred maintenance is a common way sellers understate near-term capital needs.
  • Insurance and cargo claim history: Review general liability, cargo/valuation claims, and workers' comp history. Moving is a physically demanding, damage-prone business, and claim patterns can reveal training or supervision gaps.
  • Driver retention and CDL availability: Identify how many drivers hold current CDLs, whether they're W-2 employees or contractors, and whether key drivers are staying post-sale. Driver shortages are an industry-wide constraint, and losing experienced crews shortly after close can directly reduce capacity.
  • Lead source concentration: Confirm what share of jobs come from third-party lead platforms (which charge per-lead fees that compress margin) versus direct/referral business, and review the trend over time.
  • Entity vs. asset purchase and authority transfer: Confirm whether the deal preserves existing DOT/MC authority and safety history, or whether you'll need to apply for new authority post-close.
  • Storage and warehouse obligations: If the company offers storage-in-transit or long-term storage, verify the warehouse lease terms and any customer storage contracts that transfer with the sale.

Financing a moving company purchase

SBA 7(a) loans are the most common financing path for moving company acquisitions, with the truck fleet and any real estate providing collateral. Expect lenders to scrutinize the DOT safety rating and fleet condition closely, since both directly affect insurability and operating risk. Seller financing is also common, particularly for owner-operator-run independents, since it signals the seller's confidence in the account base and driver team holding through the transition. Equipment-specific financing can supplement an acquisition loan if the fleet needs near-term replacement.

What makes a good moving company acquisition target

Not every mover is worth buying at any price. The best acquisition targets have: (1) a clean DOT safety rating with no open compliance issues; (2) a well-maintained fleet verified by independent mechanical inspection; (3) diversified revenue across commercial, residential, and specialty moves rather than one-off residential jobs alone; (4) a stable driver and crew roster with low turnover; and (5) direct/referral business that isn't overly dependent on third-party lead platforms.

Red flags: a poor or deteriorating DOT safety rating, an aging fleet with obvious deferred maintenance, revenue concentrated in a short peak season with weak off-season cash flow, high driver turnover or reliance on last-minute day labor, and heavy dependence on paid lead platforms with no direct customer relationships.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a moving company cost to buy?

Small local operators (2–5 trucks) commonly sell for $150,000–$600,000, priced at 2x–3x SDE. Established regional companies with interstate authority and a larger fleet can sell for $500,000–$2M+, depending on fleet size and commercial account base.

What makes a moving company valuable to buyers?

Repeat commercial and corporate-relocation contracts are the biggest value driver. A well-maintained fleet, clean DOT safety rating, stable driver roster, and diversified revenue beyond residential local moves all support higher multiples.

Is it better to buy a franchise moving company or an independent one?

Franchises offer brand recognition and centralized lead generation but carry ongoing royalty fees and a franchisor approval process. Independents offer more flexibility and no royalties, but rely more on the owner's local reputation and direct commercial relationships.

Where can I find moving companies for sale?

BizBuySell lists both independent moving companies and franchise resales nationwide. Franchise-specific resale boards, industry associations, and moving-industry brokers are also common sources, particularly for off-market deals.
