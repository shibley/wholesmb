How to Buy a Staffing Agency: The Complete Playbook

Staffing is one of the few service categories where a buyer can acquire meaningful cash flow with almost no physical assets — no trucks, no inventory, no real estate. What you are actually buying is a book of client relationships, a recruiter team that can fill orders, and a working-capital machine that pays workers weekly while waiting 30 to 60 days to get paid. That last detail is the one most first-time buyers underestimate, and it is the reason profitable staffing agencies can still run out of cash while growing. This playbook covers spread-margin economics, what agencies cost, how to finance one, exactly what to inspect, and the honest pros and cons.

⚡ Quick Verdict

Buy a staffing agency if…

You want an asset-light business with high operating leverage, you can fund or arrange a payroll facility on top of the purchase price, and you're comfortable in a relationship-driven sales business where retaining recruiters and clients through the transition is the whole job.

Think twice if…

The agency's gross profit is concentrated in one or two clients, its placements are almost entirely at-will with no contractual commitment, or you haven't budgeted separately for the weekly-pay/monthly-collect cash gap that scales with growth.

The economics of a staffing agency

Staffing runs on spread: the difference between the bill rate charged to a client and the fully loaded pay rate of the placed worker, where "fully loaded" includes wages plus payroll taxes, workers' compensation premium, and any benefits. Revenue is a misleading headline number in this category because it includes every dollar of pass-through wages; gross profit is the real measure of business size. A $10 million agency at a thin light-industrial spread can be a smaller business, in profit terms, than a $4 million specialized healthcare or skilled-trades agency.

Below gross profit, the cost structure is mostly recruiter and salesperson compensation plus a modest office and applicant-tracking software stack. That creates real operating leverage — incremental placements on an existing client drop through at high margin — but it also means the business is fundamentally people-dependent. Direct-hire placements, where the agency earns a one-time fee as a percentage of first-year salary, carry very high margin but are lumpy and cyclical, so they should be underwritten more conservatively than temporary billings.

What does it cost to buy a staffing agency?

Small owner-operated agencies with roughly one to a few million dollars in annual billings commonly trade from $250,000 to $1.5 million, typically priced around 3x–4x seller's discretionary earnings (SDE). Larger agencies with diversified client bases, specialized niches such as healthcare, IT, or skilled trades, and documented recurring order volume often command 4x–6x EBITDA or more. Deals in this category frequently include an earnout or holdback tied to client retention, because both sides know how quickly at-will relationships can move — a buyer should generally welcome that structure rather than resist it.

Critically, the purchase price is not the whole capital requirement. You also need funded working capital to cover the payroll cycle from day one, and that need grows as the agency grows.

Financing a staffing agency acquisition

Staffing deals typically use a two-part capital stack — one facility for the acquisition, another for payroll:

  • SBA 7(a) loans — the usual route for owner-operator-scale acquisitions, generally requiring 10–20% down for buyers with relevant staffing, recruiting, or management experience.
  • Invoice factoring or a payroll funding line — extremely common in staffing, where a lender advances against client invoices so you can make weekly payroll without waiting on 30–60 day terms. Price this in as an ongoing cost of doing business, not an emergency measure.
  • Seller financing with an earnout — well suited to this category because it keeps the seller economically motivated to hand off client and recruiter relationships intact.
  • Asset-based lending — larger agencies with clean accounts-receivable aging can borrow against the receivable base at better rates than factoring.

What to inspect before you buy

Because there are no hard assets to fall back on, staffing diligence is almost entirely about the durability of gross profit and the size of hidden liabilities.

  • Gross profit by client — request at least 24 months of gross profit broken out per client, not blended revenue. Concentration above roughly a quarter of gross profit in a single client is a material risk that should be reflected in price or deal structure.
  • Client agreements — determine which relationships are contractual with notice periods versus purely at-will purchase orders, and whether any contain assignment clauses that let the client walk on a change of ownership.
  • Accounts receivable aging — slow-paying clients directly widen the payroll cash gap; review aging buckets and any history of write-offs.
  • Workers' compensation experience modification rate — a high mod rate raises your loaded cost on every placement and compresses spread. Review claims history, not just the current premium.
  • Worker classification — confirm how 1099 contractors versus W-2 temporaries are classified and whether the practice would survive scrutiny; misclassification exposure is a real and sometimes large contingent liability.
  • Recruiter and salesperson retention — identify who actually owns the client relationships, review their compensation and any non-solicit agreements, and plan retention incentives before closing rather than after.
  • Applicant and candidate database — confirm ownership and transferability of the ATS data, since a live candidate pipeline is a genuine part of what you're buying.

Pros and cons

👍 Pros

  • Asset-light — no inventory, vehicles, equipment, or real estate to maintain.
  • High operating leverage once fixed recruiter capacity is covered.
  • Repeat order flow from existing clients creates quasi-recurring revenue.
  • Niche specialization (healthcare, trades, IT) commands materially better spreads than generalist work.

👎 Cons

  • Working capital intensive — weekly payroll against 30–60 day client terms.
  • Cyclical: temporary staffing demand contracts early and sharply in a downturn.
  • Most client relationships are at-will and can end with little notice.
  • Employer-of-record liabilities: workers' comp, classification, and employment claims travel with the business.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to buy a staffing agency?

Small owner-operated agencies with one to a few million in billings commonly trade for roughly $250,000 to $1.5 million, often priced around 3x–4x seller's discretionary earnings. Larger agencies with diversified clients, specialized niches such as healthcare or skilled trades, and documented recurring placements can reach 4x–6x EBITDA or more.

Why do staffing agencies need so much working capital?

Because you pay temporary workers weekly while clients typically pay invoices on 30 to 60 day terms. That timing gap means a growing agency consumes cash even while profitable, so buyers must fund a payroll line or factoring facility alongside the acquisition itself.

What gross margin should a staffing agency have?

Gross margin, or spread, is the difference between the bill rate charged to the client and the fully loaded pay rate of the worker. Light industrial and high-volume clerical work typically runs at the low end, while specialized professional, healthcare and skilled-trade placements run materially higher. Compare the target's spread by client and by niche rather than looking at a blended average.

What's the biggest risk in buying a staffing agency?

Client concentration combined with the fact that most staffing relationships are at-will rather than contractual. If one or two clients drive most of the gross profit and they can leave with little notice, the earnings you paid for can disappear quickly. Worker classification and workers' compensation exposure are the other two risks worth diligencing carefully.
