How Much Is a Daycare Worth?

Childcare centers look simple to price — a licensed capacity, a tuition sheet, a roster — and that appearance is exactly what causes buyers to overpay. Two centers with identical licensed capacity can be worth double or half of each other depending on enrollment rate, age mix, who holds the director credential, and how many years remain on the lease. This page covers the multiples that actually transact, the four inputs that move them, and a worked example you can run against any listing.

⚡ The Short Answer

Typical range

2.0x–3.5x SDE for a single licensed center, which usually lands near 0.4x–0.8x annual revenue. Licensed in-home family childcare rarely clears equipment value, because the business is the owner. Multi-site groups with a real management layer are priced on EBITDA and can reach 4x–6x.

Priced on

SDE, after deducting a market-rate director salary if the seller personally holds the director credential. Normalize first, then apply the multiple, then adjust for lease term and licensing history. Skipping the normalization step is the mistake that produces most overpriced childcare deals.

How childcare centers are priced

The method is standard: normalize earnings, then apply a multiple that reflects risk. What is specific to childcare is which normalization matters. Strip the owner’s personal expenses and one-time items as you would anywhere — then charge the business a real, permanent salary for the director role if the seller is filling it. In most states the operating license is attached to a qualified director physically on site, so that role is not optional and its cost does not go away when the seller does.

An owner-director reporting $150,000 of SDE is being paid twice: once as a return on the business and once as a wage for a job that costs $55,000–$80,000 to replace. Buyers who intend to own rather than operate must subtract that before multiplying, or they will pay a business multiple on a salary. The general mechanics are in how to value a business and SDE vs EBITDA; the income side of the same business is broken down in how much daycare owners make.

What moves the multiple

  • Enrollment rate, not capacity — The hinge. Because state ratios set a legal minimum staff count per enrolled child by age, an empty seat in a room that is already staffed costs the full teacher wage against zero revenue. A center running in the mid-nineties with a waiting list supports the top of the range; one in the seventies supports the bottom, whatever the license says.
  • Who holds the director credential — A credentialed director who is staying through and past the transition is worth real multiple points. A seller who is the director and leaving at close converts the deal into a license-transfer risk, and buyers price that accordingly.
  • Age mix — Infant rooms command the highest tuition and the tightest ratios, so they are frequently the lowest-margin square footage in the building. A center weighted toward preschool and pre-K generally earns more per seat than the tuition sheet suggests. Check the mix before assuming higher average tuition means higher value.
  • Lease term and assignability — Childcare build-outs are purpose-specific and effectively immovable, which hands the landlord leverage at renewal. Fewer than five years remaining, or a lease requiring landlord consent to assign, is a discount and sometimes a deal-breaker.
  • Licensing inspection history — Public in most states and the first thing you should pull. A clean multi-year record supports the top of the range; repeat ratio or supervision citations justify bidding below the mid-range, because parents can read those records too.
  • Staff tenure and credentials — Lead teachers with multi-year tenure are the asset that keeps families enrolled through an ownership change. High turnover means you are buying a recruiting problem and an enrollment cliff at the same time.

What pulls the price down

These are the findings that most often reprice a childcare deal between the letter of intent and closing. Each is a reason to bid below the mid-range, or to move part of the consideration into a seller note or an earnout tied to enrollment rather than paying it at close.

  • The seller is the licensed director and intends to leave at close with no transition.
  • Director and admin payroll missing from the P&L because the owner filled the role unpaid.
  • Enrollment trending down over the trailing twelve months, even if the current snapshot looks acceptable.
  • A lease with under five years remaining, or one that needs landlord consent you have not yet confirmed.
  • Open or recent licensing citations, particularly anything touching ratios or supervision.
  • Concentration in subsidy or employer-contract revenue that has not been confirmed as transferable.
  • Deferred maintenance on the playground, fencing, or fire and safety systems that inspection will force you to fix.

Worked example: a 72-seat suburban center

A center licensed for 72 children reports $1,098,000 of gross revenue and $173,000 of SDE at 91% enrollment. At a mid-range 2.7x that supports roughly $467,000, plus any owned real estate priced separately. Sanity-check it against revenue: $467,000 is about 0.43x, sitting at the low end of the 0.4x–0.8x band, which is consistent for a center where the owner is heavily involved.

Now normalize properly. The seller is the licensed director and shows no salary for it. Deduct a $68,000 credentialed director wage and SDE falls to about $105,000. At the same 2.7x the supportable price is roughly $284,000 — a $183,000 swing from one line item. That is the whole valuation, and it is why the director question comes before the multiple question.

Then look for the offsetting upside. Filling the eight open seats in rooms already staffed to ratio is close to pure margin: moving from 91% to 98% is worth roughly $82,000 of revenue and perhaps $60,000 of SDE after modest added labor, which is over $160,000 of enterprise value at the same multiple. A realistic structure here is a price near the normalized number, a seller note tied to enrollment holding through the first two quarters, and a transition period long enough to complete the license transfer cleanly.

Run the same arithmetic on any listing: divide the asking price by the stated earnings to get the implied multiple, deduct the director wage if it is missing, and then ask what about this specific center justifies its position against the ranges above. If nothing does, the price is the seller’s hope rather than the market’s.

Before you rely on any of this

Market ranges orient a first conversation; they do not price a deal. Once past the initial screen, get three years of tax returns and reconcile them to the P&L, pull a month-by-month enrollment report by age group rather than a single snapshot, request the full state licensing inspection history for the site, confirm the lease is assignable in writing, and have an accountant confirm the normalized earnings. Frequently Asked Questions

What multiple does a daycare sell for?

Single-site licensed centers typically trade at roughly 2.0x–3.5x seller’s discretionary earnings, which usually works out to about 0.4x–0.8x annual revenue. Small in-home family childcare operations often sell for little more than equipment value, because the business is the owner. Multi-site groups with a management layer already in place are valued on EBITDA and can clear 4x–6x.

Does licensed capacity determine the value?

No. Capacity is a ceiling, not a forecast. Enrollment rate and age mix drive earnings, because state staff-to-child ratios mean an empty seat in a room that is already staffed costs you the full teacher wage with no revenue against it. A 75-seat center at 96% enrollment is worth more than a 100-seat center at 78%.

What happens if the seller is the licensed director?

It is the single largest discount in the category. In most states the license is tied to a qualified director on site, so if the seller holds that role you must either qualify yourself or hire a credentialed director at $55,000–$80,000 a year. Deduct that wage from the stated SDE before applying any multiple, and expect the license transfer itself to be a closing condition.

Is the building included in the price?

Usually not. The center is priced on its cash flow with a market-rate rent assumed, and any owned real estate is a separate transaction. Childcare build-outs are highly specific — square footage per child, fenced outdoor space, compliant restrooms — so relocation is close to impossible and the remaining lease term is a genuine valuation input, not a formality.

How do licensing violations affect the price?

Materially, and they are public. Pull the state licensing inspection history for the specific site before you bid. A clean multi-year record supports the top of the range; a pattern of ratio or supervision citations justifies bidding below the mid-range and moving part of the price into a seller note, because enrollment reacts quickly when parents find those records.
