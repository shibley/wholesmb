Businesses for Sale in Vermont: Buyer's Guide 2026

Vermont runs one of the smallest but most distinctive business-for-sale markets in the country — a mix of craft-beverage producers, ski-town hospitality, maple and dairy production, and a Burlington professional-services core anchored by the University of Vermont Medical Center. This guide covers where to search, what industries dominate, what deals cost, and the Vermont-specific quirks — from Act 250 land-use review to mud-season cash flow — that shape a Green Mountain State acquisition.

⚡ The Short Version

Where to find listings

BizBuySell carries the broadest Vermont inventory, split between Burlington-area professional and healthcare-adjacent service businesses and a seasonal cluster of ski-country hospitality and retail deals around Stowe, Killington, and the Mad River Valley. Flippa and Empire Flippers cover Vermont-based online businesses, a smaller but steady category given the state's remote-work-friendly reputation.

What to expect on price

Burlington-area service businesses typically sell at 2.2x–2.8x SDE, while established craft breweries, cideries, and ski-town hospitality businesses often carry a premium tied to brand loyalty and constrained mountain-town real estate — but require careful review of shoulder-season ("mud season") revenue drop-off.

Why buyers target Vermont

Vermont has more breweries per capita than any other state, and that craft-beverage identity extends into a thriving cidery and distillery scene concentrated around Burlington, Waterbury, and the Mad River Valley — brands with genuine regional and national name recognition regularly change hands here. Winter sports anchor a second major economic pillar: Stowe, Killington, Sugarbush, and Okemo drive a ski-town hospitality and retail economy of restaurants, lodging, gear shops, and guiding services that peak from December through March. Burlington itself punches above its weight as a small metro, anchored by the University of Vermont Medical Center and a growing tech and remote-work scene that supports professional-services and digital businesses. Maple syrup and dairy remain foundational to the rural economy, supporting food producers, farm-to-table hospitality, and agritourism operations across the state.

The trade-off is scale and regulation: Vermont has one of the smallest total business-for-sale markets in the country, so buyers should expect fewer choices within any specific niche. Act 250, Vermont's statewide land-use and environmental review law, adds a real permitting layer for larger commercial projects or expansions that doesn't exist in most other states, and needs to be checked early rather than assumed away. Many rural and ski-town businesses also see a genuine "mud season" revenue dip in April and November, between ski season and summer tourism, that first-time buyers sometimes underestimate.

Most common business types for sale in Vermont

Vermont's business-for-sale market splits between a craft-production/hospitality core and a Burlington professional-services base:

  • Craft breweries, cideries & distilleries — concentrated around Burlington, Waterbury, and the Mad River Valley, with strong regional and national brand recognition.
  • Ski-town hospitality & retail — restaurants, inns, gear shops, and guiding services around Stowe, Killington, Sugarbush, and Okemo, with heavy winter-season revenue weighting.
  • Maple, dairy & food production — maple-syrup producers, dairy-adjacent processors, and farm-to-table food businesses supporting Vermont's agritourism economy.
  • Outdoor-recreation & guiding services — hiking, biking, and paddling outfitters serving both winter and summer tourist seasons across the Green Mountains and Lake Champlain.
  • Healthcare & professional services — medical billing, staffing, and consulting businesses tied to Burlington's UVM Medical Center and a growing remote-work professional base.
  • E-commerce & online businesses — a smaller but steady base of ecommerce and content-site listings from Vermont-based remote operators on Flippa.

Where to search for Vermont businesses for sale

BizBuySell is the starting point for most buyers and carries the broadest Vermont inventory of any listing platform — filter by Chittenden County (Burlington), Lamoille County (Stowe), or Washington County (Montpelier/Waterbury) to separate urban service-business deals from ski-country hospitality listings. LoopNet is useful for businesses tied to commercial real estate, particularly ski-town properties where the real estate often drives as much value as the operating business.

For digital and online businesses, Flippa covers ecommerce, SaaS, and content sites at global scale, while Empire Flippers is more curated and focuses on larger established businesses typically generating $5,000+/month in profit.

Off-market deal flow matters in Vermont's tight-knit business community — relationships with regional brokers who specialize in craft-beverage or ski-resort-area transitions often surface listings before they reach public marketplaces, and Vermont's small-town networks mean word-of-mouth deals are common.

Valuation: what Vermont businesses sell for

Pricing follows the same general framework as the rest of the U.S. — a multiple of seller's discretionary earnings (SDE). Burlington-area service businesses commonly sell at 2.2x–2.8x SDE, broadly in line with the wider New England region. Established craft breweries, cideries, and ski-town hospitality businesses often command a higher multiple given brand loyalty and constrained mountain-town real estate, but buyers should discount for concentrated peak-season earnings and confirm whether real estate is included in the deal.

Always normalize the financials yourself, and for any ski-town or agritourism business, request a month-by-month revenue breakdown rather than a single trailing-twelve-month number — a Stowe-area restaurant or Mad River Valley lodge can generate most of its annual earnings in a five-month winter window, so an annual SDE figure alone can mask a serious mud-season cash-flow gap.

Financing a Vermont business purchase

SBA 7(a) loans remain the most common financing path for established, profitable businesses. Vermont also has its own state-chartered lender, the Vermont Economic Development Authority (VEDA), which offers acquisition and expansion financing that can supplement or pair with an SBA loan — worth checking directly given Vermont's smaller pool of national SBA Preferred Lenders relative to bigger metro states. Seller financing is common for craft-beverage and Main Street deals, and it's worth confirming whether real estate is being sold separately, since ski-town commercial properties can carry significant standalone value.

Vermont-specific due diligence checklist

Standard due diligence applies everywhere, but Vermont has a few state-specific wrinkles worth flagging early:

  • Act 250 land-use review — Vermont's statewide environmental and land-use permitting law can apply to larger commercial projects, expansions, or certain real-estate-heavy deals; confirm whether the business or property already holds an Act 250 permit and whether it transfers with the sale.
  • Seasonal revenue concentration — for any ski-town or agritourism business, request a month-by-month P&L for at least two full seasons to understand true cash-flow timing and mud-season carrying costs.
  • Liquor and manufacturing licenses — breweries, cideries, and distilleries need to confirm their Vermont Department of Liquor and Lottery licenses and any federal TTB permits transfer cleanly with a change of ownership.
  • Wastewater and potable-water permits — many rural Vermont properties require a state wastewater system and potable water supply permit tied to the specific use; verify the existing permit covers the business's actual operations before closing.
  • Current-use tax status — agricultural or forested land enrolled in Vermont's Current Use program carries reduced property taxes but can trigger a significant tax recapture penalty if the land use changes after purchase; confirm enrollment status and any change-of-use implications.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many businesses are for sale in Vermont?

BizBuySell typically lists a few hundred active Vermont listings, weighted toward Burlington-area service businesses and a seasonal cluster of ski-country hospitality and retail deals.

What types of businesses sell most often in Vermont?

Craft breweries, cideries, and distilleries; ski-town hospitality and retail businesses; maple and dairy food producers; outdoor-recreation businesses; and Burlington-area professional and healthcare services are among the most commonly listed.

Is Vermont a good state to buy a small business?

Vermont offers a genuine artisanal and tourism-driven economy with strong local-brand loyalty. Trade-offs include Act 250 land-use review, one of the smallest total business-for-sale markets in the country, and a mud-season revenue dip between ski season and summer tourism.

Can I use an SBA loan to buy a Vermont business?

Yes. SBA 7(a) loans are available in Vermont, and the state-chartered Vermont Economic Development Authority (VEDA) offers additional financing that can pair with an SBA loan.
