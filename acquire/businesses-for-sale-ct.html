Businesses for Sale in CT: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Connecticut pairs affluent suburbs and coastal towns with a deep bench of insurance and financial-services expertise centered around Hartford and Stamford, which together support a steady supply of small businesses for sale — from restaurants and marine-related shops to landscaping companies and professional service firms. This guide shows you where Connecticut listings live, which industries come up most often, and how to evaluate a deal before you commit.

Where businesses for sale in CT are listed

Most Connecticut buyers start online. BizBuySell is the largest marketplace for Main Street and local businesses and carries the widest selection of CT listings, searchable by county, town, and industry. Beyond the big marketplaces, local business brokers and the broker networks they belong to handle many Connecticut deals, and some of the best opportunities never get publicly listed at all — they're found through direct outreach to owners or networking within the state's tight-knit business community. If you're open to a business you can run from Connecticut rather than one tied to a storefront, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can own from anywhere in the state.

Popular industries for sale in Connecticut

Connecticut's mix of affluent suburbs, a Long Island Sound coastline, and a major insurance and finance hub shapes what's commonly on the market:

  • Food & hospitality — restaurants, cafes, and catering operations, especially in commuter towns and along the coast.
  • Marine & coastal — marinas, boat sales and service, and other Long Island Sound-adjacent businesses.
  • Services — landscaping, HVAC, auto repair, salons, and other home-service companies serving affluent suburban markets.
  • Professional & B2B — insurance agencies, financial and consulting firms, and light manufacturing, supported by Hartford's insurance industry base and the state's proximity to New York.

How to evaluate a Connecticut listing

The fundamentals are the same as anywhere, but a few things matter especially in CT. Verify the financials against tax returns rather than the listing summary. Scrutinize the commercial lease — in high-cost suburban and coastal towns, rent can make or break a deal, and a short remaining lease term is a real risk for a location-dependent business. Confirm that licenses and permits (liquor licenses, food-service permits, marine/environmental approvals near the coast) transfer cleanly. Finally, check how dependent the business is on the current owner; relationships and local reputation don't always survive a handoff. For the full process, read our guide on how to buy a business.

Financing a CT acquisition

Most Connecticut small-business purchases are financed the same way as elsewhere: a buyer down payment combined with an SBA 7(a) loan and often seller financing. Roughly 60% of small-business sales include some seller financing, which lowers the cash you need at closing and keeps the seller invested in a smooth transition. Local community banks and SBA-preferred lenders in Connecticut are a good starting point for pre-qualification.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in CT?

The largest source is BizBuySell, which aggregates local Connecticut businesses searchable by county and industry. Local brokers and broker networks also list CT deals, and some sellers go off-market. For location-independent online businesses, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are popular for sale in Connecticut?

Common categories include restaurants and food service, marine and coastal businesses, landscaping and home-service firms, auto repair, salons, and professional and insurance-adjacent service firms centered around Hartford and Stamford.

How much do businesses for sale in CT typically cost?

Prices range widely. Many small Main Street businesses list from about $50,000 to $500,000, while larger companies or those including real estate can reach the millions. Most are priced as a multiple of earnings, commonly around 2x to 4x seller's discretionary earnings.

Should I use a business broker in Connecticut?

A broker can help you find deals and negotiate, and they're usually paid by the seller. Whether or not you use one, hire your own attorney and accountant to handle due diligence and review the purchase agreement before you close.
