Businesses for Sale in San Antonio: How to Find and Evaluate Them

San Antonio is one of the most buyer-friendly large metros in Texas, and the reason is simple arithmetic: it has big-city listing volume at prices that sit well below Austin and Dallas for equivalent businesses. A metro of this size supports deep inventory across the trades, hospitality, and healthcare services, while asking multiples stay closer to secondary-market levels. The catch is that San Antonio's economy leans on two concentrated pillars — the military and tourism — and both of them show up inside individual listings as customer-concentration risk that a casual read of the P&L will miss entirely. This guide covers where Bexar County listings live, which categories actually move, and the Texas-specific diligence — starting with base and government contract revenue — that decides whether a listing is worth its asking price.

Where businesses for sale in San Antonio are listed

BizBuySell is the primary aggregator for the region, and you should search well past the city limits. Bexar County holds the deepest inventory, but Comal County through New Braunfels and Guadalupe County through Schertz and Seguin have absorbed a large share of household growth and often list comparable businesses at lower prices. The I-35 corridor between San Antonio and Austin is effectively one continuous market for service businesses that dispatch crews, and buyers who search only "San Antonio" miss listings in San Marcos and Kyle that serve the same routes. The broker community here is active across the trades, restaurants, and medical practices, and because the buyer pool is smaller than Austin's, listings often sit long enough to negotiate properly — a genuine advantage over the hotter Texas metros. For businesses that don't need a physical Texas presence, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere.

Popular industries for sale in San Antonio

The listing mix reflects a long cooling season, a large military and healthcare employment base, a substantial tourism economy, and a position at the convergence of three interstates:

  • Trades — HVAC above all, because South Texas cooling load runs most of the year and replacement demand is steady, plus plumbing, electrical, and roofing, where hail seasons produce very uneven years.
  • Outdoor & property maintenance — landscaping, pool service, pest control, and pressure washing, all with near-year-round demand and correspondingly flatter seasonality than northern metros.
  • Hospitality & consumer — restaurants, bars, food trucks, coffee shops, and salons, concentrated downtown, along the River Walk, in Southtown, and in the Pearl district.
  • Healthcare services — home care agencies, dental practices, veterinary practices, and med spas, deepened by the South Texas Medical Center cluster.
  • Auto & logistics — auto repair, towing, trucking, and moving companies, supported by I-35, I-10, and I-37 and by cross-border freight running to Laredo.
  • B2B services — commercial cleaning, staffing, property management, accounting firms, and insurance agencies.

San Antonio submarkets to watch

The far north and northwest — Stone Oak, the 1604 loop, Helotes, Boerne on the Kendall County line — is the affluent end of the metro. Home services, medical practices, and personal-care businesses there carry higher revenue per customer and price accordingly. The northeast toward Schertz, Cibolo, and Universal City is heavily shaped by military households around Randolph, which makes for stable, recurring demand but also higher customer turnover than a civilian suburb, because the population rotates on assignment cycles.

Downtown, the River Walk, and Southtown are where tourism exposure concentrates. A restaurant or retail business in those corridors is priced substantially on visitor traffic, and that should be named explicitly in your model rather than folded into "revenue." New Braunfels and the Comal County side are the growth-plus-tourism blend, with river recreation driving a sharply seasonal summer peak in consumer businesses. The south and southeast sides are the value end of the metro, where businesses are more often owner-operated and less systematized — the profile where an operator-buyer can add real value instead of paying for someone else's already-built systems.

How to evaluate a San Antonio business listing

Start with base and government revenue concentration. Joint Base San Antonio — Fort Sam Houston, Lackland, and Randolph — anchors an enormous share of regional employment and contracting, and many local service businesses carry base contracts, subcontracts, or revenue that depends on federal small-business set-aside status. This is the single most common way a San Antonio listing looks safer than it is. Set-aside eligibility is tied to the owner and generally does not survive a change of control, and federal contracts require novation rather than transferring automatically. Ask directly what share of revenue is government or base-linked, read the contract terms yourself, and price that revenue at what it is worth if it does not follow you.

Second, model the Texas tax picture post-close, not the seller's. Texas levies no personal income tax, which materially improves an owner-operator's take-home, but the state does impose a franchise (margin) tax on most entities above a revenue threshold — verify filings are current and that the entity has been reporting correctly. More importantly, Texas property taxes are high, and a real-estate purchase commonly triggers reassessment at the transaction price, so a deal that includes the building can carry a tax bill well above what the seller's historical financials show. Have your CPA rebuild the expense line rather than inheriting it.

Third, do the ordinary work with local emphasis. Confirm trade licensing transfers: Texas licenses electricians and HVAC contractors through TDLR and plumbers through the state plumbing board, and those licenses attach to a qualifying individual rather than the company, so establish early whether you or a retained employee can hold the license. For bars and restaurants, treat TABC permit approval as its own schedule risk. Check hail-year normalization on any roofing business, because a storm year can double revenue and tell you nothing about the run-rate. Verify worker classification on landscaping, cleaning, and construction crews, where 1099 misclassification is common. And on any tourism-exposed consumer business, ask what a soft visitor year looks like. For the complete process, read our guide on how to buy a business.

Financing a San Antonio acquisition

SBA 7(a) loans are the standard route and typically cover 70–90% of the purchase price where the financials are clean and documented. San Antonio has a solid density of SBA-preferred lenders and regional banks, and the lower purchase prices relative to Austin mean a given down payment reaches a meaningfully larger business here — often the strongest argument for buying in this metro at all. Recurring-revenue businesses such as pest control, pool service, commercial cleaning, and home care underwrite especially well because their revenue is contracted and measurable. Where the deal includes real estate, pairing an SBA 504 with the 7(a) is usually the better structure, but underwrite the post-sale property tax reassessment before you decide the building is an asset rather than a cost. Seller financing is worth pushing for on any business with government or base-contract exposure: an earnout or seller note is the cleanest way to make the seller share the risk that contract revenue does not survive the transfer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in San Antonio?

BizBuySell has the deepest metro coverage — search all of Bexar County plus Comal County through New Braunfels and Guadalupe County through Schertz and Seguin, and extend up the I-35 corridor toward San Marcos for any business that dispatches crews. The broker community is active across the trades, hospitality, and medical practices, and listings often sit long enough to negotiate properly. For remote-run businesses, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are most commonly for sale in San Antonio?

HVAC leads the trades because the cooling season runs most of the year, followed by plumbing, electrical, and roofing. Landscaping, pool service, and pest control run near year-round. Hospitality and consumer businesses are the second large block, concentrated downtown, on the River Walk, and in Southtown and the Pearl. Healthcare services are deep thanks to the South Texas Medical Center, and auto, towing, trucking, and logistics are supported by I-35, I-10, and I-37.

How much do businesses for sale in San Antonio typically cost?

San Antonio generally prices below Austin and Dallas for comparable businesses. Most Main Street listings fall between roughly $100,000 and $700,000, with established multi-crew trade companies and medical practices running past $1 million. Pricing typically runs 2.5x–4x SDE, with contracted and recurring-revenue businesses at the higher end.

What should I watch out for when buying a business in San Antonio?

Check military and government revenue concentration first — Joint Base San Antonio anchors a lot of local contracting, federal set-aside eligibility generally does not survive a change of ownership, and contracts require novation rather than transferring automatically. Model Texas taxes post-close: the franchise (margin) tax applies to most entities, and property is commonly reassessed at the sale price, so a deal including real estate can carry a much higher tax bill than the seller's financials show. Confirm TDLR or state plumbing board licensing can be re-qualified under you, treat TABC approval as schedule risk, and normalize hail years on roofing businesses.
