How Much Is a Liquor Store Worth?

A liquor store is priced differently from most retail because three things are stacked on top of each other: an operating business valued on a multiple of earnings, a large inventory paid for separately at cost, and — in some states — a license that is itself a transferable asset with its own market. Miss any one of those and your offer is either far too low to be taken seriously or far too high to finance. This page works through the multiple, what moves it, and a numeric example.

⚡ The Short Answer

Typical range

Roughly 2x–3x seller’s discretionary earnings, plus inventory at cost. Long assignable leases, documented sales, and a protected trade area sit near the top; owner-run stores in saturated corridors with weak records sit near the bottom.

Priced on

SDE from verifiable records only. Inventory is counted and paid separately, real estate is appraised separately, and in quota states the license is valued separately again.

How liquor stores are priced

Start with normalized discretionary earnings: reported profit plus the owner’s compensation, plus personal expenses run through the business, plus genuinely one-time items. The gross margin does most of the work here, and it is thinner than buyers expect — distilled spirits and beer typically run in the low-to-mid twenties as a percentage, with wine somewhat better and cigarettes and lottery far worse. That means a store doing $2 million in sales is not a large business; it is a moderate one with a lot of cash moving through it. The general mechanics live in how to value a business, and SDE vs. EBITDA explains which metric a lender will use once a salaried manager runs the store.

Then stack the separate components. Inventory is added at the seller’s landed cost, counted immediately before closing, and it is a large number — frequently a third to half of the total cash required. In license-limited states the liquor license is a distinct asset with its own resale market and is conveyed under its own terms. If the seller owns the building, charge the business a market rent and appraise the property on its own rather than letting rent savings inflate the multiple. The operating side is covered in the buy a liquor store playbook, and how much liquor store owners make sets expectations on the income you are buying.

What moves the multiple

  • Quality of records — The single biggest swing factor. A store whose point-of-sale reports, tax returns, and bank deposits reconcile can be financed and sells at the top of the band. One that cannot be reconciled is a cash-buyer-only business and is priced accordingly.
  • Licensing regime — In quota states the license is scarce and valuable, which both raises the total price and protects the store from new competition. In open-issue states a competitor can open down the street next year.
  • Lease term and exclusivity — Remaining term drives SBA loan length, and a center-wide exclusivity clause that bars a second package store is worth real money. A short term with no options caps the multiple.
  • Sales mix — Margin per dollar varies enormously across spirits, wine, beer, tobacco, and lottery. Two stores with identical revenue can have very different earnings depending on the mix, so ask for department-level reports rather than a single sales figure.
  • Owner dependence — A store where the owner works sixty hours behind the counter is producing earnings that partly belong to a job. Deduct the cost of the staff needed to replace them before applying any multiple.
  • Trade area and competition — Drive the corridor and count competing outlets, including grocery and convenience stores if the state lets them sell. Check for a new center in permitting nearby.
  • Inventory health — Turns matter more than the headline value. Slow-moving and discontinued stock is not worth its cost, and you should not pay cost for it.

What pulls the price down

These are the findings that most often reprice a package store between the letter of intent and closing. Each is a reason to bid below the mid-range, or to shift part of the price into a seller note rather than paying it at close.

  • Point-of-sale reports that do not reconcile to bank deposits, in either direction.
  • An earnings claim that rests on cash sales the seller cannot document.
  • Inventory padded with slow-moving or discontinued stock valued at full cost.
  • A short remaining lease, no renewal option, or a landlord who will not consent to assignment without a personal guarantee.
  • Unpaid state excise or sales tax, which in many jurisdictions can follow the business to a new owner in an asset sale.
  • Compliance history — prior sales-to-minor citations or suspensions can complicate or block the license transfer.
  • A large share of earnings from lottery or tobacco, which carry very low margin and shifting regulation.
  • A competing store, or a grocery chain gaining alcohol privileges, already in permitting nearby.

Worked example: a $1.9M-revenue neighborhood store

A suburban package store does $1,900,000 in annual sales at a 24% blended gross margin, or $456,000 of gross profit. Reported profit is $118,000; add back the owner’s $65,000 salary, $9,000 of personal expenses, and $6,000 of one-time legal costs, and normalized SDE is $198,000. The seller asks 3x that number plus inventory, or $594,000 plus roughly $310,000 of stock — about $904,000 all in.

Now adjust. The owner works the counter roughly fifty hours a week; replacing that with two clerks costs about $46,000 a year, bringing passive earnings to $152,000. Department reports show $210,000 of the revenue is lottery and tobacco at a blended 6% margin, which is real but not worth a retail multiple, so the buyer applies 2.4x to the adjusted earnings rather than 3x. That puts the business value near $365,000.

Then the inventory count. Of the $310,000 at cost, $38,000 has not moved in over a year — discontinued labels and off-season stock — and the buyer takes it at 50% or leaves it with the seller. Inventory settles near $291,000, so total consideration lands around $656,000 against a $904,000 ask. None of those adjustments are hostile; they are arithmetic. But they only happen if the buyer gets department-level sales reports, an aged inventory report, and the bank statements before agreeing to a number rather than after.

Before you rely on any of this

Ranges orient a first conversation; they do not price a deal. Get three years of federal returns and reconcile them to point-of-sale department reports and to bank deposits — all three, not two. Pull an aged inventory report and walk the shelves yourself rather than accepting a summary value. Confirm with the state alcohol authority what the license transfer requires, how long it takes, what it costs, and whether the seller has any open compliance matters, then make closing contingent on the license actually issuing to you. Ask for a tax clearance certificate so unpaid excise and sales tax cannot follow the business. Read the lease for remaining term, options, assignment consent, personal guarantees, and exclusivity. Frequently Asked Questions

What multiple does a liquor store sell for?

Independent package stores commonly trade in the range of roughly 2x–3x seller’s discretionary earnings, plus inventory at cost paid separately. Stores near the top of that band have a long assignable lease, a defensible trade area, clean point-of-sale records, and earnings that do not depend on the owner working the register. Stores near the bottom are owner-run, sit in a saturated corridor, or cannot document sales well enough for a lender to underwrite them.

Is inventory included in the asking price?

Usually not. The standard structure in this category is a business price based on a multiple of earnings, plus inventory valued at the seller’s landed cost and counted the night before closing. That inventory is rarely small: a mid-size store often carries $150,000 to $400,000 of stock, which can approach or exceed the goodwill portion of the deal. Agree in writing how slow-moving, dusty, or discontinued stock will be discounted or excluded before you sign, because arguing about it during the count is how closings slip.

Does the liquor license add to the value?

It depends entirely on the state. In quota or license-limited jurisdictions the license itself is a transferable asset with a market price that can run from the low tens of thousands into six figures, and it is valued and transferred separately from the operating business. In open-issue states, where any qualified applicant can get a license, the license carries little independent value. Either way the transfer requires regulator approval on the buyer’s own qualifications, so close should be contingent on the license actually issuing, not on the seller promising it will.

How do buyers handle unreported cash sales?

They do not pay for them. Sellers in cash-heavy retail frequently claim the real earnings are above the tax returns, but undocumented income cannot be verified, cannot be financed by an SBA lender, and disappears the moment the seller leaves. Value the business on what the returns, the point-of-sale reports, and the bank deposits agree on. If the seller insists the number is higher, the burden is on them to produce records, and an unexplained gap between register data and deposits is a diligence problem rather than a bonus.

Should I buy the real estate with the store?

Only as a separate, separately priced decision. When the seller owns the building, the business is valued on earnings after charging a market rent, and the property is appraised on its own. Paying a business multiple on rent savings inflates the price of both. If you do not buy the property, the lease matters enormously: get remaining term, renewal options, assignment consent, and any exclusivity clause that stops the landlord from putting a competing store in the same center.
