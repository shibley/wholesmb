Businesses for Sale in Tennessee: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Tennessee combines no state income tax with two very different economic engines: Nashville's booming healthcare, music, and hospitality economy, and Memphis's role as a national logistics and distribution hub anchored by its position as a FedEx hub city. That split creates distinct buying opportunities depending on whether you want a Nashville healthcare-services or hospitality business, or a Memphis-area logistics or trucking operation. This guide covers where Tennessee listings live, which industries sell most often, and how to evaluate a deal before you commit.

Where businesses for sale in Tennessee are listed

BizBuySell is the dominant aggregator for Tennessee and carries the broadest set of listings, concentrated around Nashville with meaningful secondary clusters in Memphis, Knoxville, and Chattanooga. Nashville healthcare-services listings, in particular, often move through relationships with brokers who specialize in the sector rather than showing up broadly online — worth cultivating if you're targeting home health, behavioral health, or physician-services acquisitions. If you're open to running a business that doesn't require physical presence, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can own from anywhere in Tennessee.

Popular industries for sale in Tennessee

Tennessee's healthcare-industry cluster, logistics corridor, and entertainment economy shape a business mix that's distinct from most Southern states. The most active categories include:

  • Healthcare & home-health services — Nashville hosts one of the largest concentrations of healthcare companies in the country, supporting a deep market for home health agencies, behavioral health practices, and healthcare staffing businesses.
  • Trucking, logistics & distribution — Memphis's position as a major air and ground freight hub drives steady demand for trucking companies, freight brokerages, and warehousing operators.
  • Hospitality & entertainment venues — bars, music venues, and event businesses tied to Nashville's tourism and entertainment economy, though these often involve liquor licensing and lease-assignment complexity.
  • Restaurants — a fast-growing base supported by Nashville's population boom and tourist traffic, with restaurant acquisitions concentrated around downtown, Midtown, and East Nashville.
  • Home services — landscaping, HVAC, and cleaning businesses, all in strong demand given the state's rapid in-migration.
  • Franchise resales — Tennessee's business-friendly climate and no state income tax have attracted a dense concentration of franchise operators, creating a steady flow of resale opportunities across food service, fitness, and home-service brands.

Tennessee markets to watch by region

Nashville and its suburbs (Franklin, Brentwood, Murfreesboro) carry the deepest concentration of listings and the highest prices, driven by sustained corporate relocation and population growth. Memphis offers lower entry prices with a distinct logistics-and-distribution focus tied to its status as a national freight hub. Knoxville and Chattanooga sit between the two — steady growth, lower competition than Nashville, and strong outdoor-recreation and manufacturing bases respectively. Chattanooga in particular has drawn attention for its downtown revitalization and growing tech and manufacturing presence.

How to evaluate a Tennessee business listing

The fundamentals of due diligence don't change by state, but Tennessee has a few things worth noting. If you're evaluating a healthcare-services business, confirm all state licenses (home health, behavioral health, or facility licenses through the Tennessee Department of Health) transfer cleanly and that Medicare/Medicaid provider numbers and payer contracts are assignable — these can take months to re-establish if they don't transfer with the sale. For trucking and logistics businesses, verify DOT/MC operating authority and safety rating through FMCSA's SAFER system. Verify any liquor license transfers through the Tennessee Alcoholic Beverage Commission, and check commercial lease terms carefully in Nashville, where rents have risen sharply alongside the metro's growth. Financing a Tennessee acquisition

SBA 7(a) loans are the most common financing tool for Tennessee small-business acquisitions, typically covering 70–90% of the purchase price for businesses with solid financials. Seller financing is common as well — roughly 60% of small-business sales nationally include some seller note, which reduces the cash you need at closing. Tennessee's lack of a state income tax is a meaningful factor for buyers modeling post-acquisition cash flow, since more of the business's earnings flow through to the owner compared to higher-tax states. Nashville and Memphis both have strong bases of SBA-preferred lenders well-equipped to handle acquisition loans across the state's dominant industries.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Tennessee?

BizBuySell is the largest aggregator, concentrated around Nashville with secondary clusters in Memphis, Knoxville, and Chattanooga. Healthcare-services brokers carry many off-market Nashville listings directly. For online businesses you can run remotely, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are most commonly for sale in Tennessee?

The most active categories include healthcare and home-health services, trucking and logistics around Memphis, hospitality and entertainment venues tied to Nashville's tourism economy, restaurants, home services, and franchise resales.

How much do businesses for sale in Tennessee typically cost?

Most Main Street businesses list from about $100,000 to $600,000. Nashville healthcare-services and hospitality businesses with strong cash flow can exceed $1 million. Pricing is typically 2x–4x seller's discretionary earnings (SDE).

Do I need a business broker to buy a business in Tennessee?

Not required, but especially useful for Nashville healthcare-services deals and licensed businesses where local relationships and regulatory familiarity matter. Brokers are paid by the seller. Always engage your own attorney and CPA before closing — Tennessee acquisitions often involve healthcare licensing, liquor licensing, or transportation authority that require local expertise.
