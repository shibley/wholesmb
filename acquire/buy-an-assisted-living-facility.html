How to Buy an Assisted Living Facility (2026)

Assisted living sits in an unusual spot for small-business buyers: the demographic tailwind is about as clear as it gets, but the asset itself is a licensed healthcare operation bolted onto a piece of commercial real estate, and both halves have to be diligenced separately. Deals range from six-bed residential care homes run out of a converted house to 60-plus-unit purpose-built communities. This guide covers how these facilities are valued, how state licensing actually transfers (it usually doesn't), what census and payer mix tell you, and what to verify before you sign.

⚡ The Short Version

What you're buying

Three things at once: a state-licensed care operation, a resident census that produces monthly recurring revenue, and (usually) the building itself. The license is the part that most often controls the timeline, because in most states it doesn't transfer — the buyer applies for their own.

What it's worth

Small residential care homes commonly trade in the $500,000–$2M range, with the real estate carrying much of that. Larger licensed communities are typically priced per bed or on an EBITDA multiple, and both vary widely by state, building age, and care acuity.

The two sizes of deal — and why they're different businesses

Almost every assisted living acquisition falls into one of two buckets, and confusing them is the fastest way to misprice a deal:

  • Residential care homes / board-and-care (roughly 6–16 beds): Often a converted single-family home in a residential neighborhood, run by an owner-operator who may also be the administrator and may live on site. These are true small businesses — SBA-financeable, frequently sold by retiring operators, and heavily dependent on the owner's own labor. Zoning and neighborhood use permits matter as much as the care license.
  • Licensed communities (roughly 25–100+ units): Purpose-built senior housing with a professional administrator, department heads, a marketing function, and often memory-care beds at a higher rate. These trade as senior-housing real estate with an operating business attached, are typically brokered by senior-housing specialists, and draw institutional and regional-operator buyers.

The economics diverge sharply. In a small care home, the owner's own hours are a large share of the labor cost, so reported SDE often overstates what a hands-off buyer would actually earn. In a larger community, the marginal economics are driven by occupancy: fixed costs (building, core staffing, administrator) are largely set, so the difference between 82% and 93% occupancy falls almost entirely to the bottom line.

What an assisted living facility sells for

There is no single clean multiple for this category, and buyers should be skeptical of anyone quoting one. Pricing is driven by:

  • Real estate value: If the building conveys, the property is usually the largest single component of the price. Get an independent appraisal that reflects the property's value both in its current licensed use and in an alternative use, since a purpose-built community has a thin buyer pool if the operation fails.
  • Per-bed pricing: Larger communities are frequently quoted on a price-per-bed or price-per-unit basis. This is a comparison shorthand, not a valuation method — per-bed comps swing enormously between markets and between a 1970s converted building and new construction.
  • EBITDA multiple: Stabilized communities with professional management are usually underwritten on EBITDA, with the multiple depending on occupancy stability, market strength, and building condition. Facilities running below stabilized occupancy are often priced off a pro-forma, which shifts real risk to the buyer — underwrite trailing performance, not the seller's lease-up assumptions.
  • Owner-operator adjustment: On small care homes, normalize the financials by replacing the owner's unpaid or below-market labor with a market-rate administrator and caregiver wage. This adjustment alone frequently cuts the headline SDE substantially.

Licensing: the part that controls your timeline

Assisted living is licensed by the states, not federally, and the variation is significant — the license category has different names in different states (residential care facility for the elderly, adult care home, personal care home, and others), the required staffing ratios differ, and administrator certification requirements differ.

The critical point for buyers: in most states the existing license does not simply convey with the sale. The buyer applies for a new license or for change-of-ownership approval, and the agency reviews the applicant, the proposed administrator, and often the building itself before approving. That process can take months, and it can surface problems the seller never mentioned.

Practical implications for how you structure the deal:

  • Make closing conditional on your licensing approval, not on a fixed calendar date.
  • Start the license application as early as the state permits — often well before closing.
  • Confirm who will serve as the licensed administrator on day one. If it's the seller, you need a written transition plan; if it's you, confirm you meet the state's certification and experience requirements before you sign anything.
  • Pull the facility's full state survey and complaint history. Open deficiencies, an unresolved complaint investigation, or an enforcement action can delay or block approval, and these are public records in most states.

Census, payer mix, and rate structure

Revenue in assisted living is monthly and recurring, which is genuinely attractive — but the quality of that revenue varies. Request, at minimum, a 24–36 month census history by month, a current rent roll showing each resident's rate and care level, the move-in/move-out log, and the payer breakdown.

What to look for: occupancy trend (a facility drifting down over two years is telling you something the P&L hasn't yet), average length of stay and the resulting natural turnover you'll need to replace every year, care-level revenue (many facilities charge a base rate plus tiered care fees — verify those assessments are documented and actually being billed), and payer mix. Private-pay residents generally pay materially more than Medicaid waiver residents in states that cover assisted living, so a facility with heavy Medicaid concentration has both lower rates and exposure to state budget decisions. Neither is disqualifying, but the mix must be priced correctly.

Due diligence: what to verify

  • Survey and complaint history: Pull every state inspection report available, plus any plans of correction. Repeat deficiencies in the same area signal a systemic operating problem, not a paperwork slip.
  • Staffing reality vs. staffing schedule: Compare the posted schedule to actual payroll records. Heavy agency-staffing spend, chronic overtime, or a vacant administrator seat all mean the true cost of running the facility is above what the financials show.
  • Resident agreements: Review the standard admission agreement, rate-increase provisions, and any prepaid deposits or entrance fees, which are a liability that should be accounted for in the price.
  • Building condition and code: Commission a property-condition assessment. Fire-safety systems, sprinklers, generator capacity, and ADA accessibility are both a capital-expenditure question and a licensing question — a required upgrade can be a six-figure surprise.
  • Zoning and use permits: Especially for residential care homes in residential neighborhoods, confirm the use is properly permitted and that the permit survives a change of ownership.
  • Liability insurance and claims history: Request the loss runs. Professional and general liability in senior care is expensive, and a facility with claims history may face materially higher premiums under new ownership — get a quote for your own coverage before closing rather than assuming the seller's premium.
  • Referral relationships: Ask where move-ins actually come from — hospital discharge planners, placement agencies, online referral platforms, or word of mouth. Referral sources tied to the departing owner personally are a retention risk.

Financing an assisted living purchase

SBA 7(a) and 504 loans are both used in this category, and the fact that most deals include real estate makes 504 worth evaluating for the property portion. Lenders will focus on occupancy stability, the licensing transfer plan, and whether you or a hired administrator has the required credentials and experience — a first-time buyer with no senior-care background and no experienced administrator lined up is a hard file to underwrite. Seek lenders with specific senior-housing experience; generalist lenders often struggle with the licensing contingency.

Conventional commercial mortgages and HUD-insured senior-housing programs come into play for larger stabilized communities. Seller financing is common on small care homes, and an earnout or holdback tied to census retention through the licensing transition is a reasonable structure given how much of the risk sits in that window.

What makes a good assisted living acquisition target

The strongest targets share a few traits: stable or improving occupancy over a full 24 months, a clean recent survey history, a licensed administrator who intends to stay (or a credible replacement already identified), a building with no imminent large capital requirement, a healthy private-pay share, and referral relationships that belong to the facility rather than to the departing owner.

Red flags: a declining census the seller explains away, pricing based on a pro-forma occupancy the facility has never achieved, repeat survey deficiencies, an administrator vacancy at the time of sale, heavy agency-labor dependence, deferred maintenance on fire-safety or accessibility systems, and any suggestion that you can close and sort the license out afterward. You cannot.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does an assisted living facility cost to buy?

Small residential care homes (6–16 beds) commonly trade in the $500,000–$2M range, with much of that value in the real estate. Larger licensed communities are priced per bed or on an EBITDA multiple, and both vary widely by market, building age, and care level.

Do I need a license to own an assisted living facility?

Assisted living is licensed state by state, and the requirements differ substantially. In most states the license does not transfer with the sale — the buyer applies for a new license or change-of-ownership approval, which can take months. Confirm your state's process before setting a closing date.

What's the biggest risk when buying an assisted living facility?

The licensing change-of-ownership process, which can delay or block closing if there's an open deficiency or complaint investigation. Staffing is the close second — a facility that meets its ratios only through agency labor has a very different margin profile than its statements suggest.

Where can I find assisted living facilities for sale?

BizBuySell carries residential care home and assisted living listings, and specialized senior-housing brokers handle most larger communities. State licensing agencies publish lists of licensed facilities, which many buyers use for direct off-market outreach.
