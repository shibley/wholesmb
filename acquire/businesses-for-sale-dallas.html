Businesses for Sale in Dallas: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Dallas-Fort Worth is one of the deepest Main Street markets in the United States, with continuous corporate relocation, suburban housing growth, and no state income tax feeding both the customer base and the buyer pool. It is also one of the most competitive: private-equity-backed consolidators are actively acquiring trades businesses across the Metroplex, which shapes what gets listed publicly and at what price. This guide covers where DFW listings live, which categories move, and the diligence that matters specifically in Texas.

Where businesses for sale in Dallas are listed

BizBuySell is the primary aggregator for the Metroplex, and you should search regionally rather than by city — Fort Worth, Plano, Frisco, Arlington, Irving, Garland, and McKinney share one labor market and one customer base, so a Dallas-only filter hides most of the inventory. DFW supports one of the more active business-broker communities in the country, and in the trades a meaningful share of quality deals is worked quietly through brokers or sold directly to consolidators before ever reaching an aggregator. Building relationships with two or three brokers in your target category is worth more here than in most metros. For businesses that don't need a physical DFW presence, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere.

Popular industries for sale in Dallas

The DFW listing mix reflects a large, still-expanding suburban metro with a heavy freight and B2B services layer on top:

  • Home & commercial services — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, landscaping, and cleaning, driven by relentless suburban housing and commercial construction.
  • Trucking & logistics — trucking companies, freight brokerage, and warehousing tied to the I-35 and I-20 corridors and the region's inland-port distribution hubs.
  • Food & beverage — restaurants, bars, and coffee shops, concentrated in Uptown, Deep Ellum, Bishop Arts, and the Fort Worth entertainment districts.
  • Beauty & wellness — salons, med spas, and gyms, strongest across the affluent northern suburbs.
  • B2B & professional services — staffing, accounting firms, and insurance agencies serving one of the largest corporate-headquarters concentrations in the country.
  • Auto & storage — auto repair, car washes, and self-storage, all supported by sprawl and continuous household in-migration.

Metroplex submarkets to watch

The northern corridor — Plano, Frisco, McKinney, Allen — holds the highest household incomes and the newest housing, which means premium pricing on consumer businesses and fewer repair-driven service calls but strong install and maintenance demand. Dallas proper concentrates food, nightlife, and urban services, with the highest rents and the fastest turnover in restaurants. Fort Worth and the western side generally price lower per dollar of earnings and skew more industrial and blue-collar. Arlington, Irving, and Grand Prairie sit in the middle and carry much of the logistics and light-industrial inventory. As always with route- or service-area businesses, the ZIP codes actually served tell you more than the listing's mailing address, and a business whose crews cross the Metroplex daily is spending real money on windshield time.

How to evaluate a Dallas business listing

Start with the roll-up question. If you are looking at HVAC, plumbing, electrical, or another consolidating trade, ask directly whether the business has been shopped to private-equity-backed buyers and what happened. A clean, contract-heavy company that consolidators passed on usually has a reason — customer concentration, an unqualifiable license, deferred fleet capex, or earnings that don't survive scrutiny. That question saves weeks.

Second, separate commercial contract revenue from one-time residential work. Two DFW service companies with identical top lines can be worth very different multiples depending on how much revenue sits under recurring commercial agreements, and sellers are not always eager to break it out. Ask for the split explicitly.

Third, check the Texas-specific items. Occupational licensing for trades runs through state boards and generally follows a qualified individual rather than transferring with the entity, so confirm early who holds the license and whether they are staying. Property tax deserves attention too: Texas has no income tax and funds itself heavily through property tax, so if the deal includes real estate, model the tax at the reassessed post-sale value rather than the seller's historical bill. Finally, verify wages against a genuinely tight trades labor market instead of trusting historical payroll. For the complete process, read our guide on how to buy a business.

Financing a Dallas acquisition

SBA 7(a) loans remain the standard acquisition route and typically cover 70–90% of the purchase price where financials are clean and documented. DFW service businesses with commercial contracts underwrite well because the revenue is visible and durable, and Texas has a deep bench of SBA-preferred lenders experienced with trades and logistics deals. Where a transaction includes real estate, an SBA 504 loan alongside the 7(a) is often the better structure. Seller financing is common in the Metroplex and worth negotiating for — beyond improving your cash position, a seller willing to carry paper through the transition is giving you the most honest read available on how transferable the customer relationships really are.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Dallas?

BizBuySell has the deepest DFW coverage — search the full Metroplex, not just Dallas, since Fort Worth, Plano, Frisco, Arlington, and Irving share one market. Texas brokers work a large share of Main Street deals directly, and in the trades many quality listings move quietly before reaching an aggregator. For remote-run businesses, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are most commonly for sale in Dallas?

Home and commercial services lead by volume — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, landscaping, cleaning — followed by trucking and logistics, restaurants and bars, salons and med spas, B2B and professional services, auto repair, car washes, and self-storage.

How much do businesses for sale in Dallas typically cost?

Main Street businesses commonly list from roughly $100,000 to $850,000, with established multi-truck home-service and commercial-services companies regularly exceeding $1 million. Pricing typically runs 2.5x–4x SDE, with higher multiples where revenue sits under recurring commercial contracts.

What should I watch out for when buying a business in Dallas?

Ask whether the business was shopped to private-equity roll-ups and why they passed — consolidators are active in DFW trades. Then split commercial contract revenue from one-time residential work, confirm who holds the Texas trade license and whether they're staying, and model property tax at post-sale reassessed value if real estate is included.
