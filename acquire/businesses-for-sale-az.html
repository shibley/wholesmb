Businesses for Sale in Arizona: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Arizona has been one of the fastest-growing state economies in the country, with the Phoenix metro alone adding hundreds of thousands of residents over the last decade. That growth has fueled steady demand for home-service, hospitality, and healthcare-adjacent businesses, while a large retiree and snowbird population supports recurring, less cyclical revenue in many Main Street categories. This guide covers where Arizona listings live, which industries sell most often, and how to evaluate a deal before you commit.

Where businesses for sale in Arizona are listed

BizBuySell is the dominant aggregator for Arizona and carries the broadest set of listings, concentrated heavily in the Phoenix metro (Phoenix, Scottsdale, Mesa, Tempe, Chandler, Gilbert) with a smaller but active Tucson market. Most local brokers feed their listings there, so it's the logical first stop. Off-market deals, especially in home services and hospitality, often circulate privately through broker relationships and direct owner outreach — worth cultivating if you're targeting a specific niche. If you're open to running a business that doesn't require physical presence, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can own from anywhere in Arizona.

Popular industries for sale in Arizona

Arizona's climate, population growth, and large retiree base shape which business categories dominate the market. The most active include:

  • Home services — HVAC, pool service and repair, landscaping, and pest control (scorpions and termites keep this category busy year-round), all in high demand given the desert climate.
  • Hospitality & tourism — restaurants, resorts, and event businesses concentrated around Scottsdale, Sedona, and the Grand Canyon corridor, with strong seasonal (snowbird) demand.
  • Auto services — repair shops and car washes, which see heavy usage given Arizona's dust and heat.
  • Healthcare & senior services — home health agencies, senior care, and medical practices supporting the state's large retiree population.
  • Franchise resales — Arizona's growth has attracted heavy franchise investment over the last decade, so established territories with a proven local track record come up for resale regularly.
  • Construction & trades — electrical, roofing, and general contracting businesses riding the state's ongoing residential and commercial building boom.

Arizona markets to watch by city

Phoenix and its suburbs (Scottsdale, Mesa, Chandler, Gilbert) carry the deepest concentration of listings and the fastest-growing suburbs like Gilbert and Queen Creek often command a premium for home-service businesses given new-construction demand. Tucson offers a smaller, less competitive market with lower entry prices, appealing to buyers who want less bidding pressure. Scottsdale and Sedona carry a disproportionate share of hospitality and tourism listings, often at higher multiples given strong visitor spending. Flagstaff and the northern part of the state offer a different demographic entirely — more seasonal, tourism- and outdoor-recreation-driven.

How to evaluate an Arizona business listing

The fundamentals of due diligence don't change by state, but Arizona has a few things worth noting. Water rights and utility costs matter more here than in most states — verify any business with significant water usage (car washes, landscaping, pools) understands its long-term utility cost trajectory. Commercial lease terms in fast-growing suburbs have tightened as demand has outpaced new retail construction, so a below-market lease is a real asset. Check that any required licenses (contractor license, liquor license, cosmetology or health-related licenses) transfer cleanly or that you can obtain them through the Arizona Registrar of Contractors or relevant state board. Also assess seasonality carefully — many Arizona businesses see meaningful swings between snowbird season (winter) and summer, so review at least 24 months of financials. Financing an Arizona acquisition

SBA 7(a) loans are the most common financing tool for Arizona small-business acquisitions, typically covering 70–90% of the purchase price when the business has solid financials. Seller financing is also common — roughly 60% of small-business sales nationally include some seller note, which reduces the cash you need at closing and keeps the seller incentivized during transition. Arizona has a strong base of SBA-preferred lenders concentrated in the Phoenix metro, well-equipped to handle business acquisition loans across the state's dominant industries.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Arizona?

BizBuySell is the largest aggregator, concentrated in the Phoenix metro with a smaller Tucson market. Local brokers carry many off-market deals directly. For online businesses you can run remotely, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are most commonly for sale in Arizona?

The most active categories include home services (HVAC, pool, landscaping, pest control), hospitality and tourism around Scottsdale and Sedona, auto services, healthcare and senior services, and franchise resales given the state's heavy franchise investment over the last decade.

How much do businesses for sale in Arizona typically cost?

Most Main Street businesses list from about $75,000 to $600,000. Larger businesses with real estate, franchise territory rights, or strong recurring revenue can exceed $1 million. Pricing is typically 2x–4x seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) for small businesses.

Do I need a business broker to buy a business in Arizona?

Not required, but useful for surfacing off-market deals and navigating negotiations in the competitive Phoenix metro. Brokers are paid by the seller. Always engage your own attorney and CPA before closing — Arizona acquisitions often involve lease assignments and state licensing that require local expertise.
