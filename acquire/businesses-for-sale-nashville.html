Businesses for Sale in Nashville: How to Find and Evaluate Them

Nashville is one of the most competitive Main Street acquisition markets in the Southeast, and that cuts both ways. Sustained in-migration, no state income tax on wages, a national healthcare-company cluster, and a tourism economy that keeps consumer spending high all make the demand side genuinely strong. The same conditions mean more buyers per listing and higher asking multiples than Memphis, Birmingham, or Louisville, and they create a specific analytical trap: a lot of Nashville businesses look like they grew because the owner built something, when they actually grew because the metro added people. This guide covers where Middle Tennessee listings live, which categories actually move, and the Tennessee-specific diligence — starting with how to normalize those growth years — that decides whether a listing is worth its asking price.

Where businesses for sale in Nashville are listed

BizBuySell is the primary aggregator for Middle Tennessee, and you should search the whole region rather than Davidson County alone. Williamson County through Franklin, Brentwood, and Spring Hill is the affluent southern half of the metro and prices consumer and professional businesses accordingly. Rutherford County through Murfreesboro, Smyrna, and La Vergne absorbed an enormous share of household and industrial growth. Sumner County through Hendersonville and Gallatin, Wilson County through Mount Juliet and Lebanon, and Maury County through Columbia round out the commuter ring, and businesses there are often cheaper than their Davidson County equivalents doing similar work. Nashville's broker community is active across the trades, healthcare services, and hospitality, and competitive listings here move quickly — being slow to sign an NDA genuinely costs buyers deals in this market. For businesses that don't need a physical Tennessee presence, Flippa and Empire Flippers list online businesses you can run from anywhere.

Popular industries for sale in Nashville

The Nashville listing mix reflects a decade of household growth, a national healthcare-company cluster, a large tourism economy, and a position where three interstates converge:

  • Trades — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and roofing, with both new-construction and replacement demand, and hail and storm exposure that produces uneven roofing years.
  • Outdoor & property maintenance — landscaping, pressure washing, pest control, and pool service, with a mild but real winter trough.
  • Healthcare services — home care agencies, medical billing, dental practices, veterinary practices, and med spas, unusually deep because Nashville is a headquarters city for healthcare operators.
  • Hospitality & consumer — restaurants, bars, gyms, salons, and food trucks, concentrated downtown, in Midtown, and along the East Nashville and 12South corridors.
  • Logistics & auto — trucking, moving companies, auto repair, and towing, supported by the I-24, I-40, and I-65 convergence and the distribution base in Rutherford and Wilson counties.
  • B2B services — commercial cleaning, staffing, property management, accounting firms, and insurance agencies.

Nashville submarkets to watch

Davidson County holds the deepest inventory and the highest commercial rents, and downtown consumer businesses are priced substantially on tourism, which is worth naming explicitly rather than treating as background demand. Williamson County is the affluent end of the metro; home services, medical practices, and personal services there carry higher revenue per customer and correspondingly higher asking prices. Rutherford County is the volume-growth submarket, with a large industrial and distribution base and a university population in Murfreesboro, and multiples there tend to be more reasonable than in Davidson or Williamson for comparable work.

The outer commuter counties — Sumner, Wilson, Maury, Robertson — are the value end. Businesses there are more often owner-operated, less systematized, and priced lower, which is exactly the profile where an operator-buyer can add real value rather than paying for someone else's already-built systems. The trade-off is thinner labor pools and longer drive times on service routes. One structural note across the whole metro: because so much growth has been in-migration, businesses serving new residents — moving, home services, daycare, self-storage, gyms — have benefited from a tailwind that is not the same thing as operator skill, and you should price them on what they would do in a flat-population year.

How to evaluate a Nashville business listing

Start by normalizing the growth years. Ask for three to five years of financials, not a trailing twelve months, and separate the growth that came from more customers arriving in the metro from growth that came from higher pricing, better retention, or added service lines. This matters most in construction-adjacent trades, moving and storage, and anything downtown that touches tourism, where a peak year can be well above a durable run-rate. A seller who cannot explain their revenue growth in operational terms is asking you to pay a multiple on a demographic trend. In a market with this much buyer competition, the discipline to walk away from an over-normalized listing is the main edge a careful buyer has.

Second, confirm Tennessee tax and licensing status. Tennessee levies no state income tax on wages, which is a real advantage for an owner-operator's take-home, but the state does impose franchise and excise taxes on most business entities, and Tennessee's sales tax combined with local rates is among the highest in the country — so verify that filings and remittances are current and that the business has been collecting correctly on any taxable services. Most businesses also need a county and, where applicable, municipal business license, which is straightforward but frequently lapsed at small operators. Verify state contractor licensing separately: Tennessee licenses contractors at the state level above a project-cost threshold, and the license attaches to a qualifying individual rather than to the company, so establish early whether you or a retained employee can re-qualify.

Third, do the ordinary work with local emphasis. For bars and restaurants, confirm the Tennessee Alcoholic Beverage Commission license and any local beer permit can transfer on your timeline, and treat approval as its own schedule risk rather than a formality. Check customer concentration hard on any B2B or healthcare-services business, since a single large healthcare-system contract in this town can be half the revenue and is competitively rebid. Verify worker classification on landscaping, cleaning, and construction crews. Read the lease closely in downtown, Midtown, East Nashville, and the Gulch, where rent escalations have been steep enough to erase a business's margin over a renewal cycle. Financing a Nashville acquisition

SBA 7(a) loans are the standard route and typically cover 70–90% of the purchase price where financials are clean and documented. Nashville has a healthy density of SBA-preferred lenders and regional banks, and buyers can usually secure more than one term sheet. Recurring-revenue businesses — commercial cleaning, pest control, home care, property management — underwrite especially well because their revenue is contracted and measurable. Where the deal includes real estate, pairing an SBA 504 with the 7(a) is usually the better structure, though be aware that commercial property in Davidson and Williamson counties has appreciated enough that owner-occupied purchases pencil more easily in Rutherford, Wilson, and Sumner counties. Seller financing is worth pushing for here specifically as a normalization tool: if a seller genuinely believes the earnings are durable rather than market-driven, an earnout or seller note gives them a way to prove it and gives you protection if the growth was the metro's rather than theirs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I find businesses for sale in Nashville?

BizBuySell has the deepest metro coverage — search all of Middle Tennessee, including Williamson County through Franklin and Brentwood, Rutherford County through Murfreesboro and Smyrna, and Sumner, Wilson, and Maury counties. The broker community is active across the trades, healthcare services, and hospitality, and good listings move fast. For remote-run businesses, Flippa and Empire Flippers are the leading marketplaces.

What types of businesses are most commonly for sale in Nashville?

The trades lead — HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and roofing — followed by landscaping, pressure washing, and pest control. Healthcare services are unusually deep because Nashville is a headquarters city for healthcare operators: home care, medical billing, dental and veterinary practices, and med spas. Hospitality and consumer businesses are the other large block, along with trucking and logistics on the I-24, I-40, and I-65 corridors.

How much do businesses for sale in Nashville typically cost?

Nashville prices above the national median because of buyer competition. Most Main Street listings fall between roughly $150,000 and $900,000, with established multi-crew trade and healthcare service companies running past $1 million. Pricing generally runs 2.5x–4.5x SDE, with contracted and recurring-revenue businesses at the higher end.

What should I watch out for when buying a business in Nashville?

Normalize the growth years first — ask for three to five years of financials and separate metro in-migration from operator performance, especially in construction-adjacent and tourism-exposed businesses. Confirm Tennessee franchise and excise tax filings are current and that sales tax has been collected correctly on taxable services. Verify county and municipal business licenses, and confirm any state contractor license — which attaches to a qualifying individual, not the company — can be re-qualified under you. For bars and restaurants, treat TABC and local beer permit approval as a real schedule risk.
